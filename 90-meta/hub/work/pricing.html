Hunter asked directly for this to be evidence-checked, not decided on production-value instinct, because a second facilitator roughly doubles the delivery-time cost line above. It has been checked, and the honest finding is that the evidence doesn't cleanly decide it either way — stating that plainly is more useful than forcing a false answer.

What exists: no meta-analysis or RCT compares solo vs. co-facilitated adult workshops on engagement, learning, or satisfaction. The closest quantified analogue — K-12 co-teaching — has real data, but it's contradictory (some comparisons favour co-teaching, g≈0.28–0.40; others favour solo teaching, g≈−0.66) and the population doesn't map cleanly onto a single adult workshop session (children with disabilities, ongoing classroom instruction over weeks, not a one-off half-day). Facilitation-practice consensus (not research) puts close individual attention at 8–12 people, degrading above ~15 — MFC's 20-person cap sits above that zone, which is suggestive, not evidence about MFC's own format specifically.

What Hunter's specific reasons for a second facilitator actually are, assessed separately: smoother pacing and fielding questions without derailing the room are operational/logistics claims, not learning-outcome claims — real, but a different and smaller kind of benefit than "it improves outcomes." "Mixing it up" is supported by real evidence (unbroken lecture loses attention after 10–15 minutes) — but that's evidence for varying activity types, which one skilled facilitator alternating between talking and structured activity already achieves. It is not evidence that a second person is what produces the variation.

Honest conclusion: this is a delivery-quality and operations judgement, not something the evidence resolves for or against. Recommend treating it as such rather than waiting for evidence that doesn't exist: if Hunter's own experience running the room says pacing and question-handling genuinely suffer solo at 20 people, that's a real operational reason on its own, separate from any outcomes claim — and it should never be marketed to a buyer as "two facilitators produce better results," because that specific claim isn't supported.

1.1 The one-sentence category

MFC delivers mental fitness training: teaching and rehearsing a practised procedure a person runs on their own state, in the ordinary moments of their own life.

1.2 What that means, in the four distinctions MFC already holds

What it does What MFC does differently
Mental health support Addresses how someone is doing, treatment, support, recovery MFC trains what someone can do. Mental health is a state; mental fitness is a capability. Neither replaces the other
Psychological intervention / therapy Requires a clinician, usually a presenting problem, and takes place in a clinical relationship MFC teaches, in a group, without diagnosis, without a clinician, and without requiring anything to be wrong
Mental Health First Aid Trains a person to recognise and respond to someone else's crisis MFC trains a person to work with their own state, in the moment, and not only in crisis
Wellbeing programmes Deliver methods and content, mindfulness, gratitude, resilience sessions MFC trains the loop itself. Methods are what you might use; the loop is how you get to using anything at all

Why the first-aid distinction is live, not academic. The most significant new entrant to this market, The Working Mind, is licensed from Canada's Mental Health Commission via Mental Health First Aid International, with Australian delivery from 1 June 2026. A buyer will reasonably ask how MFC differs from it, and the answer is the third row above: that is a different job, not a weaker version of the same one. No claim is made here that any competitor's programme fails.

1.3 The differentiator, stated once

Everyone else teaches methods. MFC trains the loop. The failure the whole sector runs into is not that people lack methods, it is that awareness arrives without a usable next move. Someone notices they are about to do the thing they always do, and does it anyway. Stop–Shift–Do is three steps, in the moment, always. The routes are taught so that "shift" has somewhere to go; they are training content, not in-moment load.

(Status: this mechanism is founder practitioner judgement and a design premise. It is consistent with the transfer and implementation-intention evidence in the vault. MFC has not demonstrated it in its own participants. Do not present it as an established finding.)

1.4 Why the category is not "WHS training"

The regulated psychosocial hazards are environmental, workload, role clarity, support, exposure, bullying. Training does not remove any of them. Victoria has written this into regulation: information, instruction and training "must not be the predominant measure (subject to reasonable practicability)." MFC is bought alongside an employer's controls, never as one. This is already MFC's decided position (D-0102) and the 11 August paper had drifted off it.

D 0114 Return Session Is A Checkin Branching Design Retired

D-0114. The return session is a check-in

The decision

The return session exists because a skill decays if nobody comes back. It is a check-in, not a second lesson and not a drill.

The first twenty minutes are identical for Essentials and Foundations: what today is, the room's own picture from session one (aggregate, no names, no counts), warm reps with an opt-out, then whatever participants said they most wanted to learn or were finding hard. Then Essentials goes deeper into understanding and practice, and Foundations goes into how the team implements it together.

Retired, in full: the TEACH / PRACTICE / IMPLEMENT classification; the six modules; the five allocation rules and percentage thresholds; the eight-response floor; the fewer-than-five rule; candidate shared moments composed offline at day 19 to 20; and the private paper slip vote with its veto box and disposal protocol.

Why

Hunter, 2026-08-30, verbatim: "I don't agree with the eight response floor... It should just be got four people. This is what we're doing with you. We're here to help. This isn't about, like, we gotta keep a fuck because this is crazy or really scary. We're not trying to capture. Is it a bad workplace? Tell us, be honest. It's like, hey, what are the things that you wanna work on as a group... If we can keep things digital, we can keep things simple."

The reasoning, stated so it is not lost: the anonymity apparatus was built for a disclosure question, and the question actually being asked is a positive one. A secret ballot around "what shall we practise together" signals danger the question does not carry, and so creates the fear it was meant to manage. The scope rule is retained: anything really about workload, safety, staffing or how one person behaves is not a shared moment and goes to the enabling-conditions column as a category. This record originally claimed the scope rule alone did the protective work the ballot had been carrying. The 2026-08-30 safeguarding review found that wrong — see the addendum. A private route to remove a candidate is retained, as a spoken costless exit rather than paper.

Second reason, delivery. The retired design asked one facilitator who has never rehearsed the session to classify a cohort, choose among six modules against five rules, compose candidates offline, run a paper ballot and sequence a leader beat, inside seventy-five minutes. That is a protocol, not a service. Every conditional branch is something that can fail live in front of a first paying customer.

Retained from the retired design

The reason the return exists; the leader beat and its single question ("which one of these can you make true, and by when?", asking for a commitment and a date, not a verdict); the scope rule on what cannot be a shared moment; aggregate-only reporting; and the rule that nothing an individual wrote is visible when a leader enters.

Two safeguards attached at the same time

  1. The pre-session form goes to MFC, never to the manager. An employee telling their employer through MFC that they are struggling is a disclosure inside an employment relationship. The manager receives themes.
  2. Data shown back to the room carries no counts, at any group size. "Handovers came up, and a couple of people caught it at the time" is fine. "Two of you tried it" is not, because in a small room the arithmetic identifies people whatever words surround it. Corrected 2026-08-30 after the safeguarding review — this originally read as a wording test, which cannot catch an arithmetic failure. See the addendum.

Price and length consequence, modelled and deliberately not applied

Extending the Essentials return from thirty to sixty minutes adds about 0.75 of a facilitator hour. The Pricing Book costs Essentials at 8.25 hours; D-0113 adds 0.25 for the longer manager session, giving 8.5; this change adds 0.75, giving 9.25 hours, direct cost $671.43 and loaded $940.00 against the book's $605 and $847. Retained margin at the middle band falls from 61% to 57%; roughly $100 across the bands ($1,350 / $1,650 / $1,900) would restore it.

Not applied. The committee is asked on 31 August 2026 to adopt the current prices. This returns to them as a product improvement once built. D-0113 continues to govern what the price includes, and nothing here changes it.

Essentials moves from 2.0 to 2.5 hours of participant contact, closing the gap to Foundations from 2.6x to 2.1x, so Foundations earns its price on the depth of its teaching, the on-site return and the written report rather than on time. D-0110 forbids positioning the team-shared mechanism as an active differentiator while its approval gate is open, so the collective piece is not the commercial argument.

What this decision does not do

It does not clear delivery. The escalation and distress-response procedures still do not exist in cleared form, so no facilitator, including Hunter, can be cleared to deliver. Addendum, 2026-08-30: what the safeguarding review changed

The retirement of the classification, the six modules, the percentage rules, the paper ballot ceremony and the response floor on the team beat all stand. Three things in this record's own reasoning did not survive an adversarial read and are corrected in 121:

  1. "A sentence test replaces the response floor" was wrong. A wording test cannot catch an arithmetic failure. At three people, "two of you tried it" identifies the third by subtraction however carefully it is phrased. The fix is not a floor: it is no counts at any group size. Show what happened, never how many. That protects every room identically and never tells a small team it is too small for something.
  2. "The scope rule does the ballot's work" was wrong. They protect different people from different things. The scope rule governs what may be proposed; the ballot let one person remove a candidate without arguing for it in front of colleagues. Without a private route, dissent in a team of four is fully attributable and the predictable outcome is silence, a surviving candidate and a scheduled commitment several people privately object to. Reinstated as a spoken, costless exit ("tell me at the break, I'll take it off and I won't say who"), which is lighter than the paper it replaces.
  3. The form routing rule was right but incomplete. It needed a participant- facing boundary line, the restored promise that the organisation gets nothing about what anyone answered, a stated reader and deletion rule, and an honest statement that the form is not monitored. It also collides with 110's rule that the capture never reaches the organisation; that conflict is now stated rather than silently reconciled.

Also caught, and not covered by any of the three claims: simplification had made answering aloud the default with no opt-out, which breaches K-08 rule 6. The leader-free requirement for the team beat had been dropped. The D-0110 approval gate was cited in metadata and contradicted in the body. And the enabling-conditions column, which leaders see, had no de-identification standard beyond "no names". All are fixed in 121.

Every durable number MFC has is about the interval, not the room: intention converts to behaviour at roughly half its size (Webb & Sheeran 2006), and workplace resilience effects fall from d = 0.26 proximally to d = 0.07 distally (Vanhove et al. 2016). A return puts a facilitator inside that interval, and it is the point where every participant runs the sequence out loud and gets corrected — the feedback component of behavioural skills training, which a single block strips out.

Two things about the return that must both be said. Everyone practises out loud and gets corrected — but the correction comes from the person they are paired with and from the facilitator moving between pairs, not from a one-to-one coaching session, and MFC does not claim it is the same thing.

Honest status: that is a mechanism argument, not a measured one. Nothing tests a remote group return session. The return is proposed because it is cheap, safe, placed where the decay curves say the loss happens, and because it closes a component the literature says is required — not because anything shows it works.

Why this design

Total hours is the wrong variable; distribution of hours is the right one. MFC's research corpus searched specifically for a dose–response curve between ten minutes and half a day at a single occasion and found nothing. What it did find is that everything about durability is about the interval: decay starts immediately and is faster for cognitive, accuracy-based skills than physical ones (Arthur et al. 1998); in the adult single-session megastudy, 11 of 12 interventions raised in-room agency and 10 of 12 produced no benefit at four weeks; and supervisor support and work environment are among the strongest transfer predictors (Blume et al. 2010).

So the half-day stays intact and the new minutes go into the interval. Three specific reasons this shape rather than three or four separate teaching hours:

  1. The participant leaves day zero with the whole thing. Under a split course, the Reference Card and the three moments do not exist until week three — the two weeks of steepest decay spent holding a half-taught skill and no plan.
  2. It fails gracefully. A participant who misses the return has the whole skill and missed a reinforcement. A participant who misses session two of three has a broken skill. Same absence, very different damage — and this is the strongest argument in the design because it does not depend on any effect size being right. Stated honestly against the cut: with one return rather than two, a person who misses it now receives no facilitated contact inside the decay window at all. Graceful failure still holds for the skill; it no longer holds for the reinforcement.
  3. The spacing argument does not apply and MFC must not use it. Distributed practice beats massed practice on average, but the advantage correlates negatively with task complexity and disappears for highly complex skills (Donovan & Radosevich 1999) — which is exactly the cell a self-regulatory loop sits in. The honest justification is the transfer evidence and the missing feedback component, not the spacing effect.

What is demonstrated versus designed for, stated plainly. Demonstrated: that single-session formats reliably produce in-room gains and unreliably produce four-week gains; that decay is fast; that the environment around the person is a strong predictor of transfer. Designed for: that one facilitated return and a leader layer close enough of that gap to matter. Nobody has tested this shape, and nothing evidences that one contact inside the window is enough or that two would have been better. Zero cohorts have run.

MFC's own research found no in-house training comparator advertising a standard follow-up-and-report deliverable — but that is an absence from a bounded search, not proof that none exists.

What this comparison is not. It is not a claim that MFC works better than an EAP, an app, or MHFA. They do different jobs — an EAP is reactive access, MHFA teaches you to help someone else in crisis, an app is self-paced content. No ROI figure appears anywhere in this document, including the 10:1 figure attributed to Black Dog's own evaluation, which MFC's market research flags as possibly derived from a different study and requiring verification before use. A CFO asking "what's the return" should be told honestly that MFC cannot answer that yet, and that the measurement built into the product is how MFC intends to find out — in public, including if the answer is null.

The positioning language

The paragraph, for the website

Everyone else teaches methods. We train the loop.

Mindfulness, breathwork, journaling, gratitude — these are genuinely good practices. But they are methods: things you can do. What decides whether you do anything at all, in the moment that actually matters, is something underneath them — noticing, shifting, and choosing your next move on purpose. That is what we train, and we train it as a practised sequence rather than a library to pick from.

Mental health is how you're doing. Mental fitness is what you can actually do. We teach three steps — Stop, Shift, Do — and nobody is asked to remember more than three. The routes are taught so that "shift" has somewhere to go; the question of which one works for you is part of what we do with you, and it is a question much of this field never asks.

We come back. A half-day is where the learning happens; the three weeks afterwards are where it either survives or doesn't. So we return at day twenty-one — and what we do that day isn't decided in advance. It's decided by what your team tells us they actually need more of: more teaching, more practice, or help making it work here. We measure what changed, and we write it down for you — including when the answer is that nothing did.

The three sentences, for a phone call

"Most workplace mental health training teaches people methods — mindfulness, breathing, resilience techniques. We train the thing underneath: noticing a moment as it happens, shifting so you're workable, and choosing your next move on purpose — three steps, on your own real moments, not generic ones. The one-liner

Mental health is how you're doing. Mental fitness is what you can actually do — and it's trainable.

The rules that bind this language

  • The regulatory angle opens the conversation. It never describes the product. Every Australian jurisdiction now requires employers to manage psychosocial hazards, and Victoria's Psychological Health Regulations name training explicitly — that is a legitimate reason a budget exists and a legitimate reason to be let in the door. If a draft reads as a compliance vendor with a workshop attached, it has failed, however commercially attractive it looks.
  • Never say a competitor's programme fails or makes people worse. The honest line is about what has and has not been demonstrated: programmes reporting improved wellbeing scores or in-session strategy use have shown reception, and that is a different claim from durable behaviour change in real moments.
  • MFC's critique is one it has already applied to itself. A year ago MFC was teaching what it now critiques. That is credibility, not weakness, and it should be said out loud.
  • Universality is definitional. The same thing is taught to a corporate team, a community group and five people at a bouldering gym, because the claim is that it applies to every human being. Any positioning that only works for a corporate buyer has drifted.
  • "We can improve that likelihood" is the correct strength for the employer-facing argument. Not "we will change your culture."
  • Do not claim the loop is novel. It isn't, MFC's own founder-voice record says why, and it says why that doesn't matter.

Executive Summary

Every MFC offering is priced to clear its direct cost plus a 1.4× overhead allocation. Nothing is sold below cost. The half-day Foundations session is the revenue engine and is priced to carry the organisation. Shorter formats (Essentials, Introduction, the new one-hour digital) are priced to break even or slightly above, funded from Foundations margin and access levy where they do not.

For the first three organisations that book Foundations, we offer a case-study rate (source: the founding case-study pricing research; 26-29% discount, computed from that research — see §4 for how this relates to the 25–30% target range) in exchange for full measurement participation and permission to reference them. That rate still clears cost and holds a real margin. It ends after three sales or four months, whichever is first.

Beyond the corporate channel, community organisations access a tiered community rate, and applications for pro bono (free) delivery are considered from community groups or micro-businesses using a dedicated access fund.

  • No cohort loses its rate retroactively
  • No automatic extension is granted if the time limit fires before three cohorts have sold

Why This Mechanism Works

  • It directly answers the core gap: "we're sold on promise, not proof." This is the fastest legitimate way to get proof without abandoning the premium price generally.
  • It's self-funding: every sale clears loaded cost with a real margin (1.55×–2.38×), unlike the earlier founding-rate attempt which required an undocumented subsidy.
  • It's bounded: three cohorts or four months creates natural discipline against indefinite drift.
  • It reuses proven evidence infrastructure: MFC's existing N ≥ 20 threshold and measurement instrument, not invented criteria.

Why It's Difficult

  • It creates a visible seam: (source: the founding case-study pricing research) cohort 3 pays 26-29% less than cohort 4 for what looks like the same product. A procurement-literate buyer will ask why. The answer is transparent: it's limited to early adopters, and it ends automatically.
  • It sits close to breaking the "nothing below loaded cost" rule in spirit, even though it doesn't in letter. The rule protects MFC's floor; a rule that technically holds but is deliberately engineered down to the thinnest defensible margin for three sales in a row reads, to a skeptical reader, as finding the exception rather than respecting the rule.
  • It adds a fourth live pricing mechanism (standing, community tier, pro bono, case-study) to a document set that revised pricing twice in one day. That complexity is real and is a reputational cost, not hypothetical.

These difficulties are manageable because they are named in the offer itself. §5's copy says plainly: "first 3 organisations, then it ends." The margin is shown, not hidden. The purposes stay visibly distinct.

Case-Study Rate vs. Community Tier vs. Pro Bono — Not the Same Thing

Community tier / pro bono Founding case-study rate
Solves Access — buyer can't afford standing rate Evidence — MFC has no track record
Eligibility Community organisations, orgs under 15 staff (means-related) Any Foundations buyer, first 3–5 only (order-related)
Duration Standing, no expiry Capped: 3 cohorts or 4 months

These must be presented to buyers as two separate mechanisms, not one blurred "discount" story. A corporate buyer asking for community rate by pointing at the case-study offer, or vice versa, is a sign the distinction is not clear.

Micro-Business (2–5 Staff) — Real Segment, Priced as Reach

The 2–5 staff band is a real opportunity (Hunter's judgement: "don't usually ever get the opportunity to upskill or learn") and is priced separately because it behaves differently (releasing everyone stops trading; you cannot do it).

At $700 against $639 loaded (source: the pricing research), it returns 1.10× — cost recovery with a rounding error — and is explicitly labelled reach and evidence spend funded by corporate margin, not revenue (source: the pricing research).

What this band receives: A one-page team note handed to the room at the end of the session (what was covered, what the room committed to, the cue), plus, per the 2026-08-19 revision above, a coarse group-level report on whether the session landed and whether people generally reported learning something — no individual figures, no verbatims. They also receive the Leaders' Session and full measurement collection.
