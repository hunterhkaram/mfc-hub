# A-17 — one price for the Hour, attacked

## Bounded question

Does one price for the Hour, in person or online, stand commercially, and is there a better option?

## Position

**Stands with modification.** The conclusion is right; the rationale put to the reviewer was wrong in
a way a committee member would catch, and two figures in it would not survive scrutiny.

## Sources reviewed

- `60-projects/committee-pack-oct-2026/THE-CONSOLIDATED-STRATEGY.md`, section 4 and Appendix C
- The task packet at `.../packets/2026-09-23-head-of-commercial-one-price-for-the-hour.yml`

## Professional principles applied

A charity earning a margin is not a governance problem; an unexplained one is. A discount must buy
something named. A projected figure is labelled or cut.

## Evidence strength and limitations

Weak in the way that matters and the reviewer said so: nothing is validated against a competitor
quote or a real buyer, every margin figure is derived from the $66.10 rate, and no price has been
quoted to anyone. Its closing point is the honest one: two buyers hesitating at $600 would mean this
discussion was about the wrong variable.

## Alternatives considered

Four were put and ruled on. **Same price with something added online**: partially adopted, the cards
only. **A travel surcharge on in-person instead**: rejected as default, kept as a quoted tool for
genuinely distant work, never on the price list. **Interstate as its own price**: rejected, three
Hour prices before one is sold is the four-product catalogue arriving by the side door. **Keeping
$500 as an access decision**: rejected as written, because the discount buys nothing named; an
explicit concession on request reads better to a funder.

## Recommendation

**One price, $600, because it is one product.** Format is a delivery choice, not a tier.

What it changed in the paper:

1. **The rationale.** Justifying one price by margin is cost-plus reasoning in value-pricing clothes.
   The margin moved out of the rationale and is now stated as cross-subsidy: the online surplus funds
   in-person delivery where the trip costs more than the session earns.
2. **A fabricated loss removed.** "Discounting gives away $100 on every Hour sold" is a projection on
   zero delivered sales.
3. **Cards posted for online**, +$25. Online costs $190 and keeps $410 against $351 and $249. It buys
   the parity argument outright and closes the one buyer conversation where a single price fails:
   the client who has had both formats and can see something missing.
4. **The Hour is a door, not a destination.** The real incentive risk is not margin but that online
   is easier to sell and deliver, while being the only product with no return session and no report.
   Twenty online Hours generate no evidence at all.
5. **The manager session** is the only line a buyer can arbitrage, and it is the component that makes
   the half day work.
6. **Fuel, parking and tolls are costed nowhere**, so in-person costs more than the figures show.

## Strongest objection

That a 72.5 per cent gross margin on a charity's product, printed in its own paper, invites a
question the founder cannot answer with anything but margin. Answered by reframing it as
cross-subsidy and by pricing on the product rather than the cost. The reviewer's sharper version is
that the exposure is not at $600 but in the bands: online cost does not rise with headcount, so a
fifty-person online Hour is $1,000 against about $190. That is recorded as unresolved.

## What would change this view

A buyer conversation. Two hesitations at $600 would mean the variable under discussion was the wrong
one.

## Adjacent specialist or human boundary

## Proposed learning

Candidate: put a recommendation to a lens to attack rather than to generate. The conclusion survived,
the reasoning did not, and the defects found were in my argument rather than in the answer.
