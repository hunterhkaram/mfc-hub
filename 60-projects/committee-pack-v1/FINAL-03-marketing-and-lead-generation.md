## Executive Summary

MFC reaches people through three parallel funnels, each with a single recommended next step: organisations get Foundations, small teams get Essentials or V5, individuals get the digital one-hour session or a free Introduction.

The ladder itself is a lead-generation mechanism, tested against choice-overload literature and used with discipline: a room hears one flagship (Foundations for an organisation that can host it) and one accessible entry (the short digital session for anyone), not a confusing menu.

Lead generation happens through six channels: WHS consultants and brokers (#1), peak bodies (CCWT, NCOSS, NDS), local government/StateCover, co-working spaces, community talks and events, and Vistage peer groups. This is not a marketing budget problem — it is a relationship and referral problem. **Nothing is sold cold.** Everything starts with someone who has already met Hunter or has been introduced.

## 1. The Three Funnels — Not One Ladder

Different products need different entry points. The brief is right about this, and the research backs it up: a lone individual and a fifteen-person employer cannot be routed through the same door.

### Funnel A: Organisations (Foundations)

**Typical buyer:** HR/WHS leader in a 50–300 person NSW organisation

| Stage | What it is | What moves them forward |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Unaware** | Never heard of MFC | A channel touch: WHS consultant referral, peak-body mention, StateCover contact, a room Hunter is in |
| **2. Aware, not qualified** | Knows MFC exists but no live trigger (no inspector contact, no budget open) | External trigger: regulatory deadline, inspector contact, survey result, claim cost. MFC's job: be visible and credible when it fires |
| **3. Qualified conversation** | A trigger exists and MFC is in the room (via referral, peak-body intro, direct approach naming the trigger) | Hunter uses the demand-generation research's proven line: *"we tell you, ninety days later, whether they actually used it — and we give you that in writing, because from 1 July your controls have to be reviewed and evidenced."* Never lead with "resilience training" |
| **4. Proposal** | Org has headcount and session shape in mind | One-page pitch: price by band, measurement report, case-study rate and exchange terms (if applicable) |
| **5. Booked** | Signed, headcount confirmed, date set | Foundations delivery begins |

**Key constraint:** This buyer does not manufacture urgency. The regulation change (1 July 2026) created a real trigger, but it's still "should review" not "must act." The budget also has to open. MFC reaches them when both are true.

### Funnel B: Small Team / Community Organisation (Essentials)

**Typical buyer:** Manager in a 6–15 person team or a community organisation with budget authority

| Stage | What moves them |
|---|---|
| **1–2 Unaware/aware** | Same channels as Funnel A, but weighted toward peak bodies (CCWT, NCOSS, NDS) and community grant-funded delivery |
| **3. Qualified** | A small team with a manager who can approve $700–$1,200 (source: the pricing research) without committee/procurement — the demand-generation research calls this "single conversation, not a committee" buyer |
| **4→5** | Direct booking, often faster than Funnel A because there's no committee to clear |

**Key constraint:** This buyer has less budget authority but faster decision-making. The payoff is lower dollar value per session but higher conversion speed.

### Funnel C: Individual (V5, Introduction, Pro Bono)

**Typical entry:** A person at a co-working space, a community event, a scanned code, a conference

| Stage | What moves them |
|---|---|
| **1. Unaware** | Person at a co-working space, community event, scanned QR code, conference hallway |
| **2. Aware** | The "scan and ask a question, I will reply" mechanism (designed separately) or direct conversation with Hunter |
| **3. Interested** | They want more than conversation but are not a Foundations buyer — this is the honest destination for most people this funnel reaches. Solo founder in a co-working space is "not a Foundations buyer now or later" (source: the delivery-models research), costed as mission spend |
| **4. Booked** | V5 once it exists ($25–35/seat, source: the lead-generation funnel and delivery-models research), capped free Introduction, or pro bono by application |

**Key constraint:** This is honest reach and evidence volume, not pipeline to revenue. The delivery-models research is explicit: don't present it internally as sales pipeline.

## 2. Why the Ladder Is a Lead-Gen Mechanism

### The Problem It Solves

Hunter's own brief: *"they don't need to be the people that are paying it necessarily"* — and *"if you're a founder in a co-working space, they will never look at a ladder of services you have to qualify for; that's not fair."*

The ladder answers by offering **one clear recommendation per person**, not a confusing menu:

- **For an organisation:** Foundations
- **For a small team:** Essentials or V5 depending on setting
- **For an individual:** V5 or the free Introduction

**The whole ladder is visible.** You're not hidden from the Foundations option if you're an individual, and you're not forced into it either. But you hear one flagship (the half day "where we can really teach") and one accessible entry ("education plus a practical tool").

### Tested Against Choice Overload

The choice-overload literature says: more options *can* hurt, but only under specific conditions. Scheibehenne et al.'s meta-analysis (50 experiments) (source: the donor-model research, citing Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd 2010) found the effect is **zero on average** and depends on:

| Moderator | MFC's situation | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Preference uncertainty** | High — most people at a community event have never bought this before | Could overload |
| **Decision difficulty** | **Low — if the ladder structure is preserved** | Mitigates overload |
| **Similarity of options** | Low — a half day, 90 min, and one hour are not interchangeable | Mitigates overload |
| **Options actually presented** | **One per person** — the "how many people" question returns one answer | Mitigates overload |

**Verdict: the ladder is not the classic failure mode.** The classic failure is a nine-row price list with no recommendation. MFC's actual buyer-facing motion (per the delivery-models research and OFFERINGS) already avoids that configuration, provided it's never presented any other way.

**What this adds:** A room hears one flagship and one accessible entry, not five formats. That is the same "one recommendation, others visible behind it" structure the research supports.

## 5. The Donor Cost-Per-Person Framing

### What the Donor Hears

*"For every dollar you give, this is the number of people we can reach. This is what your money means."*

This is a legitimate fundraising mechanic — it gives a donor a concrete, checkable unit of impact. But it must be used with discipline:

**The numbers, from the delivery-models research (reused as verified there):**

| Format | $ per person (attendance) | $ per person (follow-up completion) |
|---|---|---|
| V12 Introduction, booked | ~$8 (source: the delivery-models research) | Not computed |
| V5 one-hour digital, org-booked | ~$13 (source: the delivery-models research) | ~$26 at 50% response (50% is a working assumption, unverified — the delivery-models research states no source in the pack gives a measured response rate for this format) |
| V5 one-hour digital, individual-booked | ~$13 (source: the delivery-models research) | ~$85 at 15% response (15% is a working assumption, unverified — same caveat as above; the delivery-models research labels this its worst-case scenario) |
| V1 Foundations | ~$91 (source: the delivery-models research) | ~$182 at 50% response (50% is a working assumption, unverified — see also the founding case-study pricing research's correction that no Foundations-specific response rate exists in the pack) |

### The Risk

**It invites a race to the cheapest format.** A donor optimising for cost-per-person will fund V5/Introduction over Foundations — exactly the formats MFC says teach least. **A donor whose target cost-per-person becomes the format-selection criterion is steering MFC's delivery mix by a number that was built to describe the mix, not direct it.**

**Mitigation:** Always pair the cost-per-person number with the mission statement: "We reach people at entry points that suit them, not just the cheapest ones. The metric tells us how far your gift goes; it doesn't tell us which format to prioritize."

## 6. The Lead-Gen Funnel: What Works, What Doesn't

### What Works

- **Relationship-gated.** WHS consultants, peak bodies, Vistage — none of these are cold outreach. They're "I have a contact" or "I'm in a room with them" situations.
- **One recommendation per person.** The ladder is visible, but the buyer hears one next step.
- **Free to reach.** Co-working spaces, community events, communities — these cost Hunter's time only, not paid ads.
- **Evidence-first.** The pro-bono channel (Channel 5) produces the reference material every other channel needs. It's not pipeline; it's enablement.

### What's Hard

- **Channel 1 (WHS) is the highest-value but slowest.** Direct sales, long procurement cycles, zero delivered sessions yet. This is the one that proves the revenue model, and it's also the hardest to close without proof.
- **No paid acquisition.** The demand-generation research found MFC's LinkedIn/company-page organic reach is close to worthless. Paid advertising exists but is expensive for a charity at this stage. MFC is betting entirely on relationships.
- **The individual channel (Funnel C) is the one that scales fastest but has the least revenue.** The delivery-models research is explicit: a solo founder in a co-working space is not a customer and should not be presented as one.

## 8. Message Design Evidence — Addendum, 19 August 2026

Newly screened marketing-science research — covering category entry, social norms, campaign
comparisons, and the Australian audience specifically (full detail:
`60-projects/mfc-master-strategy-v1/17-rationale-message-design-emotional-resonance.md`) —
confirms and extends what this document already gets right, and names two risks not yet addressed
anywhere in it.

**Confirms existing practice.** Section 1's "never lead with resilience training" instruction and
Section 6's "free to reach, relationship-gated" channel design both match the evidence directly:
Golder & Tellis (1993) found market pioneers who lead with an abstract category concept fail
disproportionately (Source: 10-sources/2026-08-19-golder-tellis-1993-pioneer-advantage.md — 47% failure rate; durable winners entered ~13 years after the pioneer); Grunseit et al. (2020) found
parkrun's no-budget success came from format properties — fixed time, one checkable act, social
obligation — not message content, matching this document's own channel logic
(`10-sources/2026-08-19-grunseit-et-al-2020-parkrun-format-evidence.md`).

**Two risks not currently addressed anywhere in this document:**

1. **Social-norms messaging should not be used as a standalone tactic.** A technique the pitch or
   channel copy might reach for instinctively (e.g. "most leaders are more stretched than they
   admit") has a poor evidence base — Foxcroft et al. (2015, Cochrane, 70 RCTs, n=44,958) found no
   substantive benefit; Papakonstantinou et al. (2025) found the effect drops to d=0.01 after
   correcting for publication bias — and a real backfire risk: Schultz et al. (2007) found
   descriptive-norm messages can produce the opposite of the intended effect depending on the
   audience's starting position. (`10-sources/2026-08-19-foxcroft-et-al-2015-social-norms-cochrane.md`,
   `10-sources/2026-08-19-papakonstantinou-et-al-2025-social-norms-meta-analysis.md`,
   `10-sources/2026-08-19-schultz-et-al-2007-descriptive-norms-boomerang.md`)
2. **Australian-audience permission structures should be used deliberately in buyer/public copy.**
   Self-deprecation functions as an entry token, not a style choice, given tall poppy syndrome
   (Peeters, 2004); humour is a documented, under-used asset (Source: 10-sources/2026-08-19-proudfoot-et-al-2015-humour-reframing-australian-men.md) — 41.1% of Australian men already use
   it to reframe difficult emotions (Proudfoot et al., 2015).
   (`10-sources/2026-08-19-peeters-2004-tall-poppy-syndrome.md`,
   `10-sources/2026-08-19-proudfoot-et-al-2015-humour-reframing-australian-men.md`)

**Status of the underlying evidence:** all 8 source records above are marked `draft` /
`awaiting-specialist` — verification inherited from the originating specialist reviews, not a fresh
primary-source check. Treat this section as evidence-informed guidance for the one-page pitch and
partner proposition documents (Section 4, not yet built), not as a claim that current MFC copy has
already been checked against it.
