# Design implications from the shortlisted researchers' actual findings

**Why this exists.** Hunter asked whether the 9 shortlisted researchers' actual papers contain a
specific idea MFC should build into the workshop, the manager component, the measurement, or the
prevention approach — not just a reason to contact them. This is that research, done before his
2026-08-26/27 finalisation pass on the workshop/product/measurement, so it can actually be used
rather than arriving after the decisions are locked.

**Method.** Three researchers dispatched to read actual papers (abstracts, and full text where
accessible) for each of the 9 shortlisted people, instructed to report "nothing actionable" rather
than force a fit. A fourth, independent pass then tried to refute the three strongest claims that
survived. One claim was found overstated and is corrected below. Access limits are named per item.

---

## Findings ranked by strength and actionability

### 1. Give managers a structured post-stressor reflection script — Monique Crane, Macquarie

**The actual finding:** Kho, Gucciardi, Ryan, Hazel & Crane (2023 *JOOP*, 2024 *Military Behavioral
Health*), a randomised trial at the Royal Military College (n=168, randomised by platoon). A
**supervisor-led "after action review"** — a short, recurring, structured reflection session where
the supervisor prompts: what stressor came up, how did you cope with it, how would you refine that
next time — embedded into routine activity, not run as a separate wellbeing meeting. Independently
confirmed: the supervisor-led group showed better psychological outcomes **earlier** than
training-only, mediated by *perceived supervisor support* (not the content of the reflection
itself), with mental-health outcomes converging between groups by the final measurement point
(though a workplace-performance measure stayed ahead even after that convergence).

**What this means for MFC, concretely:** MFC's manager/leader briefing currently exists to inform
managers about the workshop. This finding suggests giving managers something to *do*: a short,
scripted 2–3 question reflection prompt to run briefly and recurrently with their team after the
workshop (not a new meeting — folded into an existing check-in), asking about a moment someone
handled well or badly and what they'd try next time. Expect the payoff to show up early (immediate
post to 3-week) rather than assuming it matters most at 3 months — which is useful for interpreting
MFC's own follow-up data, not just for design.

**Confidence:** High on the mechanism and finding pattern (independently verified). Full published
text of the 2026 *Journal of Personnel Psychology* companion paper was paywalled; the RMC studies
that supply the concrete mechanism were confirmed via abstract/summary, not full methods tables.

**Outreach angle:** Genuine — "we read the Royal Military College after-action-review studies and
built a version of that into our manager materials" is a real, specific, honest thing to say to Crane.

### 2. Measure whether the skill was actually used, not just whether it's known — Batterham/Jackson, ANU

**The actual finding:** Jackson, Calear, Batterham et al. (systematic review, JMIR 2023, plus a
companion qualitative study). Across studies linking **skill enactment** (did you actually use the
strategy) to mental-health outcomes, most found a significant positive association; across studies
linking mere **knowledge acquisition** to outcomes, none did. Participants themselves described
training content as "a good little refresher" — the knowledge wasn't new — and attributed any real
change to *ongoing practice*, not the training event.

**What this means for MFC, concretely:** check whether MFC's 3-week and 3-month follow-up already
asks a genuine behavioural-frequency question — "have you actually used [named technique] since the
workshop, how often, in what situation" — as distinct from a knowledge-check or confidence-rating
item. If the current instrument leans toward "do you remember/feel confident about the technique"
rather than "did you use it, and when," this is direct evidence that the enactment question is the
one that will actually track outcomes, and is worth being explicit that MFC's own IMTTAQ Training
Transfer subscale is already pointed the right direction — this is a validation of an existing
choice as much as a new one.

**Confidence:** High — independently confirmed against both the systematic review and the
qualitative study.

**Outreach angle:** Genuine — this is literally why MFC measures transfer, not just satisfaction,
and can be said to Batterham directly.

### 3. A cheap, scripted week-1/week-3 manager touchpoint, because a one-off session doesn't supply what sustains uptake — Eva Kyndt, Deakin

**The actual finding:** Wijga, Beausaert & Kyndt (2025), a systematic review of 73 studies on what
drives workplace learning uptake. The antecedents are consistently *ongoing/embedded* conditions —
supportive and participative leadership feedback, psychological safety, coaching access, task
autonomy — not one-off inputs. The review's own frame is learning "in the flow of work," and does
not itself directly test transfer from a single discrete training event (a genuine limit, not a gap
in the research pass).

**What this means for MFC, concretely:** MFC's current design is one workshop plus one manager
briefing — structurally, it supplies none of the "ongoing" condition this review's antecedents point
to. The cheap fix: a scripted 2-minute manager check-in prompt at week 1 and week 3 ("ask your team
what they've tried from the workshop"), built into the existing manager-briefing materials, so an
ongoing element exists without redesigning the workshop itself. Worth stating plainly: this also
means MFC's existing IMTTAQ "Opportunities to Integrate" subscale is already measuring in the
direction this review points to — another case of confirming an existing choice, not just adding one.

**Confidence:** Medium — abstract/summary-level read, not full text; applying a general
workplace-learning review to justify a change to a discrete-training-event design is a reasonable
inference, not a direct finding from the paper itself. Verify against full text before treating as
settled.

**Outreach angle:** Genuine, and doubles as the actual reason to approach her — MFC's follow-up data
is a live test of the antecedents her review can only theorise about from cross-sectional literature.

### 4. Split the manager briefing into "notice/respond" and "prevent/design the environment" — Aimée Gayed, UNSW/Black Dog

**The actual finding:** HeadCoach (the only manager-training programme in this space with a positive
RCT on behaviour change — Gayed et al. 2024, *MJA*, cluster RCT of physicians supervising residents)
is structured as two distinct behavioural tracks, each taught in short (~10 min) scenario-based
units: **responsive** skills (notice, ask, support help-seeking, modify work, support return) and
**preventive** skills (manage job demands, control, support, role clarity, communication). The trial
found significant improvement in supervisors' self-reported behaviour, confidence and knowledge,
durable to 4–6 months.

**The important limit, stated by the authors themselves:** the trial did **not** measure outcomes
for the people being supervised — only the supervisor's own reported behaviour change. There is no
evidence from this trial that training the manager improved the team's mental health.

**What this means for MFC, concretely:** if MFC's manager/leader briefing is currently one bundled
block, restructuring it into an explicit "notice and respond" segment plus a separate "prevent and
design the environment" segment mirrors the only positive-RCT design in this space. **Also concrete:
do not let any MFC marketing or grant language imply "train the manager, protect the team"** — the
best evidence here explicitly can't support that causal chain, only "manager behaviour and
confidence improves."

**Confidence:** High — full text read for both the protocol and the 2024 trial.

**Outreach angle:** Genuine — "we split our manager briefing into responsive and preventive tracks
after reading HeadCoach" is specific and true if implemented.

### 5. Use scenario/case-based framing, not definitions, when teaching what a harmful pattern looks like — Annabelle Neall, Flinders

**Corrected after independent verification** — the first-pass claim overstated the paper's
recommendation; corrected version below.

**The actual finding:** Keenan, Masser, Neall & Barlow (2025, *Employee Relations*, n=320) found that
giving people a definition of sexual/gender harassment — legal, psychological, or none at all — had
**no significant effect** on whether they recognised the behaviour as harassment or rated it as
harmful. **The authors do not recommend dropping definitions** — they recommend definitions be
*retained but embedded within* a broader approach: policy dissemination, climate surveys, narrative
and case-based training, bystander intervention. Addition, not replacement.

**What this means for MFC, concretely:** if any MFC content teaches "what counts as" a harmful
workplace pattern by presenting a definition and assuming that alone builds recognition, this is
direct evidence that definitions alone don't move recognition or harm-perception — but the fix is to
pair a definition with a short scenario/case example ("notice this pattern"), not to drop the
definition. This is a genuinely good methodological fit with MFC's own "moment" framing (a concrete
scenario, not an abstract term), so it's a real, usable design point even after the correction.

**Confidence:** High on the finding, corrected and reverified on the recommendation.

**Outreach angle:** Genuine but should be stated accurately — "your finding on definitions alone not
building recognition matches why we teach through scenarios" is honest; "we dropped definitions
because of your paper" would not be, and shouldn't be said.

### 6. A narrow, real talking point: role clarity as protective against burnout — Karina Jorritsma, Curtin

**The actual finding:** Hon, Sun, Li, Jorritsma & Parker (2025), a latent-profile analysis of
Australian construction workers, found the work-design profile with strong relational resources and
manageable demands had significantly lower burnout, while low-role-clarity/high-demand profiles
(concentrated among younger workers) had the highest.

**What this means for MFC:** narrow but real — role-clarity as a specific, evidence-linked prompt for
managers to check with their team (distinct from generic "support wellbeing" language) is cheap to
add to the manager briefing. **Thrive at Work itself is not a transferable design** — it's an
organisational audit-and-tracking methodology, a different kind of tool from a one-off workshop, so
"adopt its structure" isn't coherent advice; only this one talking point is.

**Confidence:** Abstract-level only; full text paywalled.

### Researchers who yielded nothing actionable beyond general topical relevance

- **David Cheng, ANU** — the assumed 2024 paper turned out to be specifically about leader laughter
  under challenge (a narrower phenomenon than "leader-subordinate emotional dynamics" generally),
  and full text was inaccessible. The "self-control at work" body of work assumed in the brief
  doesn't appear to exist as a current, Cheng-led programme — one hit was a minor 2010
  co-authorship on a different topic. **No design change recommended on this basis.**
- **Mark Lee, RMIT** — only topic labels (self-determination theory, ACT) are visible on his profile;
  no actual paper, abstract or finding could be located behind them. **Do not cite his SDT/ACT work
  as if it were a specific finding** — there is no verified paper behind the label.
- **Peter Caputi's 2015 Delphi study** — doesn't specify workshop mechanics, only construct
  boundaries ("mental fitness" = modifiable capacity to utilise resources/skills to flexibly adapt).
  Nothing here is inconsistent with MFC's framework, and it's a genuinely useful citation if MFC ever
  needs to justify its measurement approach to a funder or academic reviewer — but it's a
  positioning/citation asset, not a design change.

## What to actually do with this before tomorrow

This is research, not a decision — none of it has been built into anything yet. If it's useful for
tomorrow's finalisation pass, the concrete, buildable items are:

1. A short scripted manager reflection prompt (post-stressor: what happened, how did you handle it,
   what would you try next time), for recurring informal use — Crane.
2. Check whether the 3-week/3-month follow-up asks a genuine behavioural-frequency question ("did you
   use X, how often") rather than only knowledge/confidence — Batterham/Jackson.
3. A scripted 2-minute manager check-in at week 1 and week 3 — Kyndt.
4. Split the manager briefing into a "notice/respond" segment and a "prevent/design the environment"
   segment — Gayed, with the explicit caution not to claim manager training protects the team's
   mental health.
5. Pair any "what counts as a harmful pattern" content with a scenario, not a bare definition — Neall
   (corrected).
6. One role-clarity talking point for the manager briefing — Jorritsma.
