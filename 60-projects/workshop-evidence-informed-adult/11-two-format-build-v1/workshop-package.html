Name the distinction — 0:25–0:33K-04 locked wording

What's taught: the actual locked definition, in three exact sentences — "mental fitness is how well we navigate life's everyday moments"; "it shows up in how we think, work with what we feel, act on what matters, and connect with ourselves and others"; "mental health is how we are doing, mental fitness is what we are able to do." Teach the gap — 0:33–0:45

What's taught: that nobody is born knowing how to handle every human moment — these are skills learned through guidance, observation, experience, practice and repetition, and most people assembled whatever strategies they have from whatever life happened to give them. The explicit reframe: "that does not mean we are broken... it is a gap in what we were taught, not a gap in us." How: stated directly, with an optional aggregated "where did you learn this" data point if the approved baseline form supports it — never invented. The emotional arc is deliberately sequenced: recognition, then relief, then possibility — never shame. Why it's here: the single highest-risk beat in the whole day for the "you're weak, you didn't train" misread the fitness metaphor invites. Get this specific beat wrong and everything after it is heard as blame, not education.

Thoughts, feelings, what's trainable — 0:45–0:55

What's taught: not every thought is a fact; feelings aren't good or bad, they carry information but aren't instructions; the locked line "the Passenger is not bad"; a feeling arriving is not a failure, and the session isn't about forcing a feeling away — what's practised is what happens next. How: stated directly, with an explicit rule not to call any named feeling universally useful or harmful — function explained without universalising anyone's experience. Why it's here: this is the non-adversarial foundation the actual tool (taught in Block 2) depends on — without it, "Shift" risks being misread as suppressing a feeling rather than creating space around it.

Give the day a destination — 0:55–1:00

How: stated directly, closing Block 1 before the break. Why it's here: sets the honest ceiling for the whole day before any content is taught, not after — the same claims discipline (K-08) that governs everything else MFC says.

BLOCK 2 — The tool and what shapes access — 1:15–2:15

The five skills — 1:15–1:22K-04/K-05 locked wording

What's taught: the full public capacities, in exact locked language — "Notice — notice what is happening inside you, around you, and between you and others"; "Shift — create enough space to become workable, through your body or your thinking"; "Choose — identify what matters and follow through"; "Focus — put your attention where it matters and return it when it drifts"; "Connect — connect better with yourself and others." Plus the explicit "workable does not mean calm, and you don't need all five in every moment." How: full sentences read from the card, unparaphrased, with one concrete example per skill, capped at three minutes total for all five. Why it's here: gives "mental fitness" concrete shape as five distinct capacities, at a length that can actually teach them properly (the shorter formats only name them).

Stop–Shift–Do — 1:22–1:32

What's taught: the actual tool, in exact locked wording — "Stop, catch the moment. Shift, create enough space where useful and available. Do, make the next move with intention." Then the colour scaffold and its three corrections: red isn't freeze, it's catching the moment; amber isn't calm down, it's creating space (sometimes sharper or more connected, not settling); green isn't the right answer, it's the next move made with intention. How: read in full locked wording, colour explicitly named as a memory aid only ("not instructions from a road"), each misreading corrected in the same breath it's introduced. Why it's here: the actual intervention — the specific tool everything else in the day is built around.

Worked example & retrieval — 1:32–1:44Priya, added 2026-08-19; non-desk alternative added 2026-08-20

What's taught: the tool applied to one concrete scene — Priya's Tuesday, mid-morning, an email lands that needs a careful answer: Stop (notices the pull to fire back immediately), Shift (feet flat, reads it again), Do (writes down the one real question, answers only that). Then unaided retrieval: "what were the three steps," "which step created space, which caught the moment," ending on the red/amber/green corrections one more time. How: told as a small scene, not a checklist — a single running character, not an isolated example, so the content is followed rather than just listed. All response modes offered (aloud, point, write, gesture), nobody cold-called. Why it's here: retrieval practice, not re-exposure, is what actually strengthens what's retained — hearing the tool once and being able to produce it are different things. Status: the desk-worker email scenario doesn't land for trade, healthcare, frontline or warehouse audiences — a fully scripted non-desk alternative (same four beats, a mid-shift trigger instead) now exists; facilitator reads the room and picks, not yet tested with a real non-desk cohort.

Human Operating System — 1:44–1:53full three-part model

What's taught: a teaching model for "what is shaping what's available to me right now" — the Driver (what you can reach: noticing, attention, pausing, choosing, your next move), the Passenger (thoughts, feelings, urges, memories, how you read a situation — with the locked line "the Passenger is not bad"), the Engine (body state: energy, tiredness, tension, breathing, hunger, sleep). How: stated as an explanatory model, not a diagnosis or a thing to memorise — Pathways, Navigation and Environment are deliberately not added as participant terms at this length. Why it's here: gives the room language for why the skill sometimes doesn't feel reachable, without that becoming a personal indictment — protects against the same blame-misread risk Block 1's gap beat already named.

State Check — 1:53–1:59

What's taught: the locked question "is my state too much, too little, or workable for this moment?" — too much means downshift, too little means upshift, workable probably needs no shift, and "workable does not mean calm." How: the question is stated, then run privately, immediately, ten seconds, eyes open or closed, nothing written or shared — no show of hands, colour, score or label. Why it's here: this is the trigger that decides whether the tool gets reached for in a real moment at all.

Mental Reps — 1:59–2:08

What's taught: the locked definition — "a mental rep is catching a moment and choosing your next move with intention... the outcome does not determine whether it counts" — plus the reframe that they've already done versions of this (e.g. catching yourself scrolling and putting the phone down), and the honest naming of what's actually missing: nobody named it, taught the shift, or helped practise retrieving it while life was happening. How: stated directly, with an explicit caution not to claim repetition guarantees capability, and not to say people have done "thousands" of these as a factual claim. Why it's here: names the actual unit of practice before asking anyone to do one — without this, "practice" stays an abstract instruction rather than something with a defined shape.

Complete one small rep — 2:08–2:15moved earlier, corrected 2026-08-13/17

What's taught: nothing new — applies the tool to one ordinary moment from today or later today (a neutral invented example is explicitly allowed instead of real material). Catch the moment, run a State Check, choose one small shift or next move. How: four minutes private, then an optional three minutes in pairs (pass, stay private, or use the neutral example — no explanation needed), facilitator does not roam or listen in. Why it's here: a real, if small, "I did that" inside the room, this early in the day — self-efficacy from an actual completed rep is what makes the rest of the day feel worth the remaining time. Waiting until Block 3 for the first real attempt was the design's own earlier flaw, already corrected.

Break — 2:15–2:30

What's taught: nothing. How: restart time stated; shift materials and the contrast route prepared for Block 3. Why it's here: the second structural reset in the day.

BLOCK 3 — Use it and carry it into life — 2:30–3:34

Train in easier moments — 2:30–2:40

What's taught: the gym analogy — you wouldn't start with the heaviest thing in the room, you'd train with something you could move; the same applies here. The easy rep isn't a fake rep, it's the training ground, the same way most skills (language, an instrument, driving) become more available through practice, without guaranteeing what happens in the hardest moment. How: stated directly, with an explicit caution against the old "this takes no extra time" framing — attention and effort still cost something real, even in moments that already exist. Why it's here: directly targets the belief that only hard moments are worth practising on — that belief is what stops people practising at all between sessions.

Four kinds of shift — 2:40–2:46

What's taught: a shift can work through four places — body (stand, release shoulders, eat, a slower out-breath), attention (widen to the room or narrow to one task), perspective (test whether a thought is fact, try a more generous reading), connection (ask for two minutes, sit near someone, seek appropriate support) — plus the explicit line that connection only applies where it's safe and appropriate, and some problems need a boundary or system change, not a better individual response. How: one ordinary example per category, issued as a reference sheet. Why it's here: widens the menu for "Shift" without turning it into a prescription list the room has to memorise.

Try two shifts — 2:46–2:52

What's taught: nothing new — two of the four routes (normally one body, one attention) actually tried live, briefly, with a genuine opt-out to just watch instead. The explicit line: "nothing is useful information too — a strategy not suiting you is information about the strategy, not a failure in you." How: led one at a time, notice before, notice what changed (if anything) after — never intensified to manufacture an effect. Why it's here: a mastery experience, not just a description, for at least two of the four shift routes before the day's peak moment.

The same moment twice — the engineered peak — 2:52–3:04named 2026-08-19/20

What's taught: nothing stated directly — this is shown, not told. One scene, run twice: same person, same ordinary pressure, same ending, only the response differs. Afterward, the explicit line: "the outcome can stay the same and the rep can still count. This is not a story about a person being fixed. It is one moment handled differently." How: the approved film or a rehearsed two-facilitator scene (never participant volunteers), played with full performance commitment, not underplayed — followed by a genuine five-to-ten-second held silence before any discussion, then "what did you notice?" with observation invited before interpretation. Why it's here: peak-end research (Kahneman, Fredrickson & Schreiber) — people remember the most intense moment of an experience far more than its average; this beat already had the dramatic shape to carry that, it just needed to be treated as the peak on purpose. Status: a non-qualified safeguarding-adjacent read found the scene itself acceptable (ordinary pressure, not acute distress) but flagged that a held silence in a room that may include someone already struggling deserves an explicit rehearsal check before being trusted.

Introduce Radar; choose the route — 3:04–3:10

What's taught: a Radar is "a short, active list of moments you are choosing to notice, practise with and return to right now" — moments to handle better and moments to experience more fully. Two routes exist: Route A (routine — straight to private individual moments, used when no separately approved team module exists) and Route B (only when an exact, privacy-reviewed team nomination was collected in advance — de-identified themes only, private and anonymous response, no show of hands, no majority vote). How: Route A is the default; Route B requires its own separately approved gate and is never manufactured in the room. Why it's here: makes explicit that any collective element is a genuinely separate, approved thing — not something invented on the spot to look participatory.

Write a private Radar — 3:10–3:16D-0010, exactly three moments

What's taught: "think about the person you are trying to become in this season of life," then three personally meaningful moments — handle-better or experience-more-fully — each with one possible next move. Explicitly: "three is an MFC pilot choice, not a scientifically exact number," blanks allowed rather than forced. How: private writing, modelled with one neutral first-person example, never a participant's story, facilitator stays at the front, never circulates behind anyone. Why it's here: your own approved standing decision — one moment risked being too thin to generate a genuine plan; three gives real material to choose from at the next beat.

Choose one cue — 3:16–3:22

What's taught: pick one moment, not the whole page, and attach it to something already in the week — the kettle, unlocking your phone, a seatbelt click. Explicitly named as "a visibility support, not a contract — missing it does not break a streak and does not mean you failed." How: stated directly, with a caution against describing this as habit-stacking that will create a habit — it's an unvalidated support choice, not a guarantee. Why it's here: the object the day-21 return actually checks whether someone used — the same implementation-intention mechanism as the shorter formats.

Make return normal — 3:22–3:27tone rewritten 2026-08-20, substance re-verified

What's taught: "you're going to forget... all of that is completely normal, none of it is failure," plus the honest naming that sleep, movement, food, health, workload, support and environment all shape what's actually available — "not everything that gets in the way is yours to solve alone." How: delivered warmly, directly ("real talk"), not as a clinical caveat — with an explicit rule that "one rep is enough" is never framed as an efficacy claim, only as a complete valid attempt for today. Why it's here: a participant who reads a lapse as failure stops trying — this is what keeps the door open to trying again.

Close the teaching — 3:27–3:30weighted alongside the peak, 2026-08-20

What's taught: "you're leaving today with the start of something real, not a finished skill... this was never a course to complete. It's a practice you get to come back to... today was your first rep here, not your last chance. I'm glad you came, and I mean that." How: a real pause before moving on, the close deliberately weighted to carry the same emotional attention as the 2:52 peak — not a brisk wrap-up. Why it's here: peak-end research says the ending carries disproportionate memory weight alongside the peak; engineering only one of the two would leave the other to chance. A non-qualified safeguarding-adjacent read found this specific line group-directed and appropriate, not a professional-boundary risk.

Why this matters — the gap, and who it costs

Most of us were never sat down and taught this. We're trained for money, driving, cooking, a career — the skills that let us function as a role — but many adults get little or no structured help with the skills actually in control of how we experience our own life: working with a thought, working with what we feel, choosing what we do next. That's not a gap in any one person in the room. It's a gap many people were left with, not a deficit in them, and it's part of why so many are quietly surviving their moments instead of navigating them.

It doesn't stay a private cost. Untrained, it shows up as burnout, friction nobody names properly, conflict that escalates because nobody had a smaller move available, an already-stretched mental-health sector catching problems only once they've compounded. Trained — one moment at a time, the same way physical fitness is built one rep at a time — this is a contribution hypothesis, not a demonstrated outcome, toward a team, a family, a workplace that reacts to pressure and one that functions better under it. The organisational and community case is the same hypothesis at a larger scale: the ambition is fewer costly moments handled badly, over time — conditional on real reach, faithful implementation, ongoing access, safety, support and distribution, none of which this single session establishes on its own.

The journey — hook, teach, apply

Three blocks, three jobs: Block one earns the reason to care, Block two builds the tool and the confidence to use it, Block three hands it over. That arc — motivation before mechanism, mechanism before application — is the spine everything else hangs off.

Five curiosity loops, by design

0:00
Opens on a question, not an answer

The first words out of the facilitator's mouth are "why are we here," not a housekeeping list. An open question is an open loop — the room is curious before it's told anything.

0:07 – 0:33
Generate something safely, then see the pattern named

Participants privately form associations before hearing MFC's definition. This uses self-generation to create recognition; the current evidence supports the principle, not a guarantee that this exact activity will be remembered better by every MFC participant. Shared writing is optional and separately gated.

0:33 – 0:45
An unanswered question hangs since the arrival form

"Where did you learn this?" was asked in writing before the session started, never addressed until now. Aggregating the room's real answers live closes a loop that's been open since they walked in.

2:52 – 3:04
Anticipation, resolved by watching, not being told

"Watch the same moment happen twice" sets up curiosity about what will differ. The answer arrives through demonstration — show, not explain — which is also why the two versions end identically: the tension is in what changed, not in whether it worked out.

3:08 – end
A loop left open on purpose

The Radar write is private, never read out, never collected — the one loop the session deliberately doesn't close. It goes home with them, which is the point: the session ends but the reflection doesn't have to.

Why the pacing changes mode roughly every 10–15 minutes

Every block alternates teach and experience in fairly short stretches rather than one long lecture followed by one long activity. Sustained lecture-style attention is commonly cited as dropping off noticeably somewhere in the ten-to-eighteen-minute range without a change in what the room is doing — the exact number is debated in the literature, but the direction (attention decays without a mode change) is not — a mode switch (talk → write → talk → drill) resets it, rather than fighting it with longer single blocks. The two fifteen-minute breaks land at the three real act-breaks in the story — end of "why," end of "how," before "now go use it" — not at arbitrary clock intervals.

What the design draws on

Retrieval practiceworked example + reconstruction

Actively recalling material is generally better supported for retention than passively rehearing it. The Half-Day uses one neutral worked example followed by spoken, written, gestural or silent reconstruction; the exact MFC activity remains a testable design.

Generation effectprivate recognition

Self-generated material can support memory compared with material only received. The private recognition activity uses that principle before MFC names its definition; it does not claim a guaranteed effect or require visible group contribution.

Implementation intentionscue selection

Attaching a plan to a specific, concrete trigger ("when X, I will Y") is a well-evidenced technique for converting intention into actual follow-through, distinct from motivation alone. The cue-card beat is exactly this pattern.

Self-determinationopt-out, private writes

Self-determination theory names three needs — autonomy, competence and relatedness. This design leans specifically on autonomy: real, visible choice, nothing forced, as a core driver of genuine motivation rather than surface compliance. Every private-write beat and the standing opt-out rule protects it on purpose. (Competence is addressed separately, via guided practice and self-efficacy below; relatedness isn't a deliberate design target of this session.)

DBT / CBT / ACTadjacent skill traditions

Stop–Shift–Do and the mental rep have structural similarities to established skills traditions, including distress-tolerance patterns, cognitive reappraisal and acceptance alongside values-directed action. These are adjacent influences, not evidence that MFC's integrated loop is clinically equivalent or validated.

Transfer of trainingreal-life reps, not the room

Added 2026-08-05. Skills taught in a training room routinely fail to transfer to real-world use unless the practice context resembles the real one (Baldwin & Ford's training-transfer research). This is the mechanism-level reason Mental Reps are designed as practice inside the actual moment — a difficult conversation, a rising feeling — rather than rehearsal in the classroom. It's arguably the single most direct piece of evidence for the whole premise: the workshop exists to close a knowing–doing gap, and this is the literature on why that gap exists and what closes it.

Habit formationthe mental rep, repeated

Added 2026-08-05. Beyond the initial cue-and-plan (implementation intentions, above), habit-formation research (Wood & Neal) finds that repeated action inside a stable, real context — not motivation alone — is what builds an automatic response over time. Each Mental Rep is one repetition inside that mechanism; the "practise in easy moments so it's there in hard ones" framing in the session is this principle stated in plain language.

Self-efficacyguided practice, live

Added 2026-08-05. Bandura's work identifies mastery experience — actually doing the thing, even in miniature — as the strongest driver of the belief that lets someone attempt something new, distinct from motivation (self-determination theory, above) or intention (implementation intentions). The in-session guided practice is a deliberate small mastery experience before participants are asked to try it alone.

Adult learningrecognisable relevance

Adult-learning traditions support making learning relevant to real adult contexts. This design combines neutral worked examples with private participant-selected moments so relevance does not require disclosure. It is a design philosophy, not efficacy evidence for the exact workshop.

Pluralistic ignorancethe aggregate reveal, 3:04

Added 2026-08-09. Groups routinely already want a change and don't know it — each person privately assumes they're the only one (Prentice & Miller). Anonymously surfacing what the room actually nominated beforehand, rather than asking people to volunteer opinions live, corrects this directly and is why the reveal happens before any discussion, not after.

Collective implementation intentions"when X, we will Y"

Added 2026-08-09. A shared "if-then" plan agreed together outperforms a shared goal stated as an aspiration (Thürmer, Wieber & Gollwitzer) — the same mechanism as the individual cue card at 3:16, applied to a group commitment rather than a personal one. This is why the shared moment, when one is agreed, is phrased as a concrete trigger and action, not a general direction.

Normative-conduct disciplinenever a forced consensus

Added 2026-08-09. Descriptive-norm messaging can backfire (Schultz et al.) — stating or implying "not everyone wants this" risks reinforcing exactly that. This is why the beat explicitly permits the room to decline a shared moment out loud, rather than quietly pressuring toward one: a nominally-agreed moment from a room without real appetite would be worse than none, and would risk stating the low-uptake norm aloud.

Measuring access, not moodthe primary outcome item

Added 2026-08-12. The measurement design has its own construct reasoning, separate from the teaching design above. The primary question asks how often someone remembered to use what they knew while the moment was happening — access under load — rather than how they felt, and holds "I didn't know what might help" and "there wasn't a relevant moment" off the scale entirely, so no-opportunity is never scored as failure. The same logic drives the two-week window and the deliberately unflattering top option: a question most people can already answer "almost always" to at baseline has ceilinged and can show nothing. The in-room "looking back now" item exists for a different reason again — response shift. The session teaches the meaning of the phrase the question uses, so a genuinely improved participant can rate their own before-state lower afterwards; a then-test asked in the room is the only way to see that happening rather than mistaking it for no change.

What this is and isn't. The frameworks above are established, separately-evidenced bodies of work MFC's design draws structural inspiration from — not a claim that this specific workshop has itself been clinically tested. It hasn't; no pilot has run yet, and one of D-0016's six substantive delivery protections — safeguarding — still has to clear before one can, per D-0099 (the formal strategy/pricing/build-order gate itself is superseded; the six delivery protections remain open). Where this page says a design choice "can help" or "supports" something, that's tied to the named mechanism above, not a guarantee of outcome for any individual participant.

Why 90 days, honestly: the evidence gives a defensible zone, roughly two to six months, and doesn't pick an exact point inside it. Nobody should be told 90 days is scientifically optimal for a single half-day session. Not earlier than about ten weeks, because habit-formation research finds a median of around 66 days to near-automatic use for a simple daily habit — and MFC's behaviour is harder than that on every dimension. Not later than about six months, because the closest comparable Australian brief-training research still shows a detectable effect at six months and is genuinely unclear beyond it.

The strongest parts of this design

The primary item, paired with the then-testForm 1 + Form 2

One matched question carries the whole comparison, and the "looking back now" item in the room exists specifically to catch the way the session changes what the question means to the person answering it. Reported together rather than one replacing the other — if the two agree, that is a stronger result than either alone.

A harm check on every formunconditional

Not conditional, not routed past, and paired with "Made things harder" sitting openly on the helpfulness scale. An instrument with no route for harm cannot detect harm — this one has two.

The sequencing decided in advancethree forms, then a fourth

Day 21 for whether the teaching held, day 90 for whether the practice did, and both written down before any cohort runs. Fixing the interval in advance removes the option of picking a flattering month after seeing the data.

Analysis rules written before any datacounts, floors, no per-situation testing

The primary statistic, the reporting floor, and the conditions under which a rise should be read as form-familiarity rather than real change are all fixed now. A later null result reads as honest because the rules predate it.

The real gaps — where this is genuinely weak

The primary question has never been tested with a real personthe single biggest unknown

Every judgement about whether it is too easy, too hard, or ambiguous is reasoned, not proven. If people can't reliably tell "Rarely" from "Sometimes," the scale needs fixing before use freezes it. Testing it with five to eight people is a few hours and no money — it is the highest-value next step by a distance, which is why it sits first on the list.

The harm-check wording has no clinical ownera live gap, not a formality

Nothing board-approved covers where responses are stored, for how long, or how they are destroyed. There is also no approved rule for handling free-text answers — removing identifying detail, and what gets escalated to whom — and five items depend on that rule existing.

No proven way to deliver Form 3delivery, not design

The day-21 send has never actually worked once. The day-90 wave is committed to in writing but deliberately not switched on until it has.

BLOCK 1 — Terms, disclosure, the gap — 0:00–0:14

Welcome & the six controls — 0:00–0:03ESSENTIAL, full length, not summarised

What's taught: the six controls stated in full: presence isn't agreement; private writing stays private; opt-out any time, no explanation ("I'll sit this one out" is a complete sentence); this is education, not therapy or treatment; two short anonymous forms explained upfront; accommodations (move, stand, step out, headphones, write instead of talk) need no permission. Online adds two more: camera stays optional all hour; no private room is assumed, writing in your head is a complete version of any activity. How: delivered as the very first thing said, before any content, in full — not trimmed because the session is shorter. Why it's here: a sent, sceptical room that feels controlled disengages before content starts; removes the default assumption a mandated audience walks in with.

A genuine light beat — 0:00–0:03added 2026-08-19, FLEXIBLE, facilitator's own words

What's taught: one honest, self-deprecating, unscripted aside, placed after the six controls land and before the disclosure statement. How: facilitator's own words, matched to the room, not scripted verbatim — the one addition in this beat that may be dropped under time pressure without reporting it as a change. Why it's here: same reactance-management function as Essentials' and Foundations' equivalent beats — genuine warmth right after necessary process content, before anything substantive starts.

The safety-exception disclosure — 0:03–0:05ESSENTIAL, D-0016, must not be rushed

What's taught: the one honest exception to "nothing you write is seen" — that a room like this can sometimes bring up something that isn't everyday, and that isn't a failure of the session or of them. Participants are pointed to a card section headed "if something is troubling you." How: delivered slowly, not mumbled — the facilitator waits until people have actually looked down before continuing. Why it's here: a legal and ethical requirement; doing it before anything else means nobody discovers the limit of privacy after they've already relied on it. Status: the actual referral content behind the card is an unresolved placeholder reserved to qualified humans under D-0016 — not cleared for a live participant session in either mode.

Pre-measure — 0:05–0:07ESSENTIAL, D-0017

What's taught: nothing new — two items completed, one self-efficacy item and one intake item. How: anonymous, no names, collected face-down into a single pile and not read in the room; online per §B7. Why it's here: the only honest way to find out whether the session did anything — this is the "before" the immediate and day-21/day-90 measures compare against.

Self-elicitation, compressed — 0:07–0:12ESSENTIAL, NON-CUTTABLE, the highest-value opener

What's taught: a 90-second private write — top of the card "what comes to mind when you hear 'mental health'?", underneath "what comes to mind when you hear 'mental fitness'?" — no definitions given first. How: cards collected face-down, shuffled in front of the room, then 2-3 read aloud unattributed and flatly (never "whoever wrote…"); anything personal rather than a word-association is skipped, not read. Why it's here: the generation effect — producing your own answer before being handed a definition makes it stick harder; the 0:12 reveal depends entirely on the room having produced this material first.

Reveal and reframe, merged — 0:12–0:14ESSENTIAL, K-04/K-05/D-0005, densest compression in the format

What's taught, in full and in order: the locked definition — "mental health is how we are doing, mental fitness is what we are able to do" and "mental fitness is how well we navigate life's everyday moments, shown in how we think, work with what we feel, act on what matters, and connect with ourselves and others"; the confrontation ("who taught you what to do at 2am when your brain won't stop?") held in three seconds of genuine silence, not rescued early; the permission ("nobody did… there's nothing wrong with you, you were just never shown"); the D-0005 context-neutral framing, one explicit sentence, that this session assumes nothing about their life outside the room; the single underlying line that thoughts aren't facts, feelings are information not instructions, and what's trainable is what you do next. How: four dense elements, two minutes, no decoration, rehearsed with a timer. Why it's here: this is the single highest-risk moment for the "you're weak, you didn't train" misread the fitness metaphor invites — get it wrong and everything after is heard as blame.

BLOCK 2 — The tool, the drill, the model, the check — 0:14–0:47

The five public skills — 0:14–0:18ESSENTIAL, LOCKED WORDING

What's taught: the five full locked sentences — Notice (notice what's happening inside you, around you, between you and others), Shift (create enough space to become workable), Choose (identify what matters and follow through), Focus (put attention where it matters and return it when it drifts), Connect (connect better with yourself and others). The seven deeper foundational skills are deliberately not shown to the room. How: read once, no elaboration beyond one short line each, full sentence taught not the one-word label. Why it's here: gives "mental fitness" concrete shape without spending limited time drilling five skills when only Stop-Shift-Do needs to be retrievable today.

Stop, Shift, Do & the colour scaffold — 0:18–0:22ESSENTIAL, D-0054, locked wording

What's taught: Stop (catch the moment), Shift (create enough space where useful and available), Do (make the next move with intention), in locked wording; the traffic light named exactly once, as contrast, then the three corrections delivered from memory as the actual teaching, not caveats: red is catching, not freezing; amber is making space, not calming down (workable is the goal, not calm); green is your next move made with intention, not the right answer — outcome doesn't decide whether it counted. How: corrections must come from memory, not read; SSD card issued and turned over. Why it's here: D-0054 governs this beat — at this length there is no later contrast activity to recover the point if it's taught wrong, so the corrections are delivered as the teaching itself, not an addendum.

The call-and-response drill — 0:22–0:32ESSENTIAL, PROTECTED, D-0004, the largest single allocation

What's taught: the three steps retrieved out loud by the room, corrected in real time, both directions (mapping and corrections), full mechanics and explanatory-feedback table in §A5. How: active, low-risk — everyone can answer, point, write or gesture, nobody singled out; not tested silently. Why it's here: retrieval practice, not re-exposure, is what the evidence says actually sticks — hearing it once does not equal producing it later.

Human Operating System — three components — 0:32–0:38ESSENTIAL framing, FLEXIBLE depth, Driver/Passenger/Engine only

What's taught: deliberately compressed from six parts to three — the Driver (access to awareness, attention, pausing, choosing), the Passenger (every thought, feeling, urge, impulse — "the Passenger is not bad," some of the loudest are keeping you safe), the Engine (body state — energy, tension, breath, sleep, hunger; "half of what people call a bad mood is an Engine problem wearing a costume," worded audience-neutrally per A-22's 2026-08-20 correction). The diagnostic question landed hard: "what is shaping access in this moment?" — not "what's wrong with me." Pathways, Navigation and Environment named in one sentence and explicitly not taught. How: "not a diagram of your brain, not a diagnosis" stated directly; if someone starts diagnosing themselves or an absent third party with it, redirected once, lightly. Why it's here: gives the tool a "why this happens" layer so the skill doesn't feel like willpower alone — explanatory job is to make one question askable, not to be reproduced.

State Check — 0:38–0:41ESSENTIAL, K-07 lock-in, a distinct construct — not a fourth step

What's taught: "is my state too much, too little, or workable — for this moment?" — too much (come down toward workable), too little (come up toward workable), workable (enough access to choice; workable does not mean calm — calm is one flavour and the wrong one for a job interview). How: explicitly stated as not part of Stop-Shift-Do if asked; no colours, no scale, no Mood Meter framing anywhere — a standing D-0054 boundary independently required by K-06/K-08. Why it's here: the trigger that decides whether the tool gets reached for in a real moment — without it, Stop-Shift-Do has nothing telling someone when to use it.

Live State Check, on themselves, right now — 0:41–0:43ESSENTIAL, NON-CUTTABLE

What's taught: nothing new — the room actually runs the check on their own real state, right now, in the room. How: ten seconds of counted (not eyeballed) silence, never answered out loud, never written down, never collected or scored; facilitator does not scan faces. Why it's here: the only beat in the format that trains in-the-moment noticing of the participant's own state rather than a scenario — "that was a Stop. That's it. You just did it sitting in a chair."

Mental reps, normalised — 0:43–0:47ESSENTIAL, K-07 locked definition

What's taught: the locked definition verbatim — "a mental rep is catching a moment and choosing your next move with intention… the outcome does not determine whether it counts"; the scrolling example (hands up if you've caught yourself scrolling and put the phone down — "that was a rep"); the callback to the words written at 0:07 ("something you build" — that's what gets built). What does NOT count if asked: a moment alone, an autopilot habit, reading about mental fitness without applying it. How: no new content — paraphrase allowed in delivery but the last locked sentence said exactly. Why it's here: reframes something they already do, unnamed, as a trainable skill — "nobody told you it was a skill you could get better at. That's the only thing that changes today."

BLOCK 3 — Radar, the cue, commit, close — 0:47–1:00

Radar: one moment, participant's choice of strand — 0:47–0:54ESSENTIAL, D-0010, three named deviations from the 3-hour

What's taught: the locked prompt — "think about the person you are trying to become in this season of life"; both strands offered equal weight, a moment to handle better or one to experience more fully, participant picks one; the hard line that a shared/team Radar must never turn a workload or unsafe-leadership problem into someone's personal skill deficit; the facilitator models one full moment aloud (moment, what usually happens, one possible next move) before the room writes their own. How: private, silent, five-minute write, never shared aloud, never collected — the facilitator stays at the front and does not circulate (circulating during a private write is surveillance regardless of intent); the two most common stuck points ("my moment is too big" / "I can't think of one") are voiced mid-write as the only rescue anyone gets; explicitly stated: one moment is a complete outcome, and a blank slip is allowed. Why it's here: the one place a participant's own real material appears, and it stays theirs — carries the three named D-0014 deviations from the 3-hour format (one moment, participant's choice of strand, shared moments named but not worked).

Cue selection — the engineered peak — 0:54–0:56ESSENTIAL, NON-CUTTABLE, D-0008, named 2026-08-19 as the session's deliberate peak

What's taught: that the problem isn't motivation, it's that in the actual moment nothing from today will be remembered — so the plan anchors to something that already, physically happens every time (the key in the door, sitting at the desk, the kettle, the seatbelt click), written as one sentence: "when [the thing that already happens], I will [Stop / State Check / my one next move]" — kept small enough to act on in about ten seconds. How: written individually; facilitator circulates lightly here (unlike the Radar) to help with "what already happens," offering suggestions not assigning them; once written, fold the card in half once and hold it closed for a second in real, unfilled silence — added 2026-08-19 as the beat's physical close. Why it's here: this is the single mechanism the entire post-session pathway routes through — implementation-intention evidence (Gollwitzer & Sheeran) is specific to exactly this structure. Status: the fold-and-hold addition was not costed into the beat's original two-minute allocation in a format with almost no slack anywhere — flagged as a real open timing risk pending stopwatch rehearsal, not a settled two-minute beat.

The non-requirement beat, and lapse-and-return — 0:56–0:58ESSENTIAL, NON-CUTTABLE, split out deliberately so it can't be lost to time pressure

What's taught: this does not require extra time — no ten minutes a day, no morning routine, no app, the moments are already happening; one rep is enough as a starting point; and the honest bit — most people's first attempts fade, that's expected and not failure, and the way back in is the same as the way in, one rep, any moment, starting whenever you notice you stopped. How: stated plainly, "I mean them literally, not as reassurance." Why it's here: lapse-and-return is on the never-cut list specifically because it used to sit inside the format's most compressible beat, making it the first thing lost when a facilitator ran late.

Close — 0:58–0:59ESSENTIAL, STANDALONE, NOT A PITCH, scripted guardrail

What's taught: "you've got the distinction, the handle, and one moment to try it in. That's the whole ask… nobody taught you this. That was true when you walked in and it's still true — the difference is that now you know it was a gap in the teaching, not a gap in you." The 3-hour format may only be mentioned after this close, briefly, flatly, never as the payoff they were missing. How: scripted, not ad-libbed — live warmth is exactly where claims boundaries leak; explicit list of banned outcome claims ("you'll feel better," "you've built a habit," "you're mentally fitter than when you walked in") never said. Why it's here: protects the claim MFC can actually stand behind — durable change was never promised in an hour; the decision to start practising is what the hour is actually for.

End-of-session measure — 0:59–1:00ESSENTIAL, D-0017

What's taught: nothing new — two items only (not the 3-hour's four), anonymous, same as the opening measure. How: given its own slot so it's neither rushed nor allowed to overrun the finish time; remaining measurement items move to the follow-up outside session time. Why it's here: the "immediate" point in the baseline → immediate → day-21/day-90 measurement chain — without it there's no same-day comparison point at all.

Why this matters — the gap, and who it costs

Most of us were never sat down and taught this. We're trained for money, driving, cooking, a career — the skills that let us function as a role — but many adults get little or no structured help with the skills actually in control of how we experience our own life: working with a thought, working with what we feel, choosing what we do next. That's not a gap in any one person in the room. It's a gap many people were left with, not a deficit in them, and it's part of why so many are quietly surviving their moments instead of navigating them.

It doesn't stay a private cost. Untrained, it shows up as burnout, friction nobody names properly, conflict that escalates because nobody had a smaller move available, an already-stretched mental-health sector catching problems only once they've compounded. Trained — one moment at a time — this is a contribution hypothesis, not a demonstrated outcome, toward a team, a family, a workplace that reacts to pressure and one that functions better under it. The organisational and community case is the same hypothesis at a larger scale: the ambition is fewer costly moments handled badly, over time — conditional on real reach, faithful implementation, ongoing access, safety, support and distribution. One hour is a real start toward testing that, not proof of it.

Why this format exists

It gets us in more doors. Cheaper to run, short enough to fit inside a lunch break, and — because it doesn't lean on the two-facilitator contrast staging or the extended shift-toolkit practice — other trained facilitators can run it, not only Hunter. That means more sessions, more often, reaching smaller businesses that could never book a half-day.

It isn't pretending to be the half-day. Nobody leaves this fluent. They leave understanding the idea, able to recall the tool, and having actually done one rep — with a clear next step if they want the real thing.

The journey, and what it's built on

Why it's built this way, not just what's in it.

The walkthrough tab is the schedule. This is the reasoning underneath it — the participant's actual journey through the hour, and why a compressed format is a legitimate design category, not a lesser one.

The journey — the same arc, at a third of the scale

Same hook-teach-apply shape as the half-day, compressed to one cycle instead of several: one curiosity loop (private write → compressed reveal), one applied moment instead of a full practice block, one Radar moment instead of three. Nothing is a watered-down version of a half-day beat — each surviving piece is taught at full strength; what's different is how many cycles there's time for.

Two curiosity loops, and one deliberately unresolved

0:07 – 0:18

Same mechanic as the half-day's opening, still short: write your own association, then hear the definition and the gap land against it. The reveal itself got longer in this pass (four minutes to eight) precisely so the loop actually closes instead of being named and rushed past.

0:38 – 0:41

The tool is taught in full at 0:18–0:26, drilled at 0:26–0:32, and then — the beat that didn't exist in the standing design — actually run, once, live. The gap between "hearing the words" and "doing the thing" used to be the whole rest of the session, unresolved. Now it closes inside the hour, in three minutes rather than four — tightened so the extra explanation time upstream didn't have to come from anywhere else.

0:41 – end

Same device as the half-day's Radar write, at one moment instead of several: private, never read out, goes home with them. The plan is complete, but the practice itself is deliberately unfinished — that's the honest promise of this format.

Why a compressed format is a real design category, not a lesser one

There's a growing body of research specifically on brief, single-session formats in mental-health-adjacent settings — not this workshop, and not this population, but directly relevant to the design principle behind offering a genuine one-hour format rather than treating it as a thin advertisement for the half-day. A 2025 review across 24 systematic reviews and 415 clinical trials found the large majority reported positive effects across a range of outcomes, and Schleider's meta-analytic work has found some single-session formats producing effect sizes not far off multi-session programs averaging far more contact time.

The important distinction. That research is from clinical intervention studies — treating anxiety, depression, and similar — in populations seeking clinical support, mostly youth. MFC's Introduction is a 60-minute non-clinical education session for general adults, not a clinical intervention, and hasn't itself been tested. It's cited here for one narrow reason: it's evidence that a well-structured single session, done properly, can produce real change — supporting the decision to design this format with genuine rigour rather than as a lesser, compressed afterthought. It is not evidence that MFC's specific workshop produces those effects.

What else the design draws on, at this scale

Retrieval practicethe drill

Same mechanism as the half-day — actively recalling the tool strengthens memory more than hearing it once. Given the least time, this still gets the largest single block of the hour.

Implementation intentionsthe cue card

The one-moment plan ends on a concrete "when X, I will Y" trigger, not just an intention — the same evidenced pattern as the half-day's cue selection, compressed to one moment instead of several.

Generation effectthe private write

Even at ninety seconds, the room writes its own associations before being told the definition — the self-generation still happens, just faster.

Transfer of trainingthe one live rep

Added 2026-08-05. Skills taught in a room routinely fail to transfer to real use unless practised in a context resembling the real one (Baldwin & Ford). The one live rep at 0:38–0:41 exists specifically because a taught-but-never-tried tool is the weakest possible version of this format — one real repetition, even brief, is worth more than a longer explanation with none.

Self-efficacydoing it once, live

Added 2026-08-05. Bandura's mastery-experience mechanism — actually doing the thing, even briefly — is the strongest driver of the belief that lets someone try it again alone. At one hour, this format can only afford one such moment; it's placed deliberately rather than cut for time.

Measuring access, not moodthe primary outcome item

Added 2026-08-12. Separate from the teaching design above: the primary measurement question asks how often someone remembered to use what they knew while the moment was happening — access under load — not how they felt, and keeps "I didn't know what might help" and "there wasn't a relevant moment" off the scale so no-opportunity is never scored as failure. The scale is built to avoid a ceiling, since a question most people can already answer "almost always" to at baseline can show nothing. The in-room "looking back now" item counters response shift: the session teaches the meaning of the phrase the question uses, so a genuinely improved participant can rate their own before-state lower afterwards — a then-test is the only way to see that rather than mistake it for no change.

Delivery mode · designed, not cleared

The same hour, delivered with nobody in the room.

The Introduction is one product with two delivery modes. Digital is not a separate, lesser design — the core session, the drill and the measurement are the same. What changes is what the format has to add to work at all, and one safeguarding condition the in-person mode does not carry. Neither mode is deliverable today, for reasons set out at the foot of this page.

What is the same

The design of record is unchanged: the same five-phase order, the same beats on the same clock, the same protected safeguarding block delivered in full at the top. Brevity is never a reason to shorten safeguarding text, and neither is a screen.

The drill0:22 – 0:32

Choral, out loud, both sets, no cold-calling. It stays the largest single practice block of the hour in either mode.

The private beatsself-elicitation, Radar, cue

Private and never shared in either mode. Nothing a participant writes is read out or collected.

The measurementlight set only

The same light instrument the Introduction carries in person. No extra items are added because the session is online, and none are dropped.

What digital delivery has to add

These are requirements, not options — they come from the design of record and apply to any online run of this format.

  • Captions and a transcript for any film or audio component. — access, not a nice-to-have
  • Camera-on is explicitly optional, and said out loud as optional rather than left to be inferred.
  • The private-write and Radar beats assume a private space the participant may not have. The script states that the writing can be done mentally, or later, without penalty and without follow-up. A participant without privacy will otherwise self-censor, and that is a design problem, not a participant problem.
  • No registration gate may collect personal data before the privacy and data-use explanation.
  • Platform-independent. The design must not depend on breakout rooms, polls or reactions — features that are access barriers as often as they are conveniences.

The gap this mode carries, stated plainly. In digital delivery, no facilitator is physically present to observe non-verbal distress. A participant can go silent, turn off camera, or close the tab, and no one can intervene, notice, or follow up in real time. The content still plausibly surfaces personal material — compression may make that more likely, not less, since there is less time to contextualise it safely.

This is not one of D-0016's original six substantive delivery-protection matters (per D-0099, D-0016's formal strategy/build-order gate is superseded; those six protections remain open). All six were written assuming a room: item 3 (emergency response) assumes a venue with an on-site owner, and there is no venue; item 5 (host responsibilities) assumes a host, and there may be no host. It is recorded as a seventh matter, unresolved and newly identified.

It is not a reason to shelve the mode, and it is not a readiness difference. No format is deliverable today. Matters 1–6 block the half-day in person, the Introduction in person, and the Introduction online equally. The digital mode clears on matters 1–6 plus 7 — that is the whole difference.

What is routed to qualified humans, not drafted here: a digital-specific distress and escalation procedure; what a remote facilitator can and cannot do; whether digital delivery requires a second person monitoring; and whether MFC's insurance position covers remote delivery at all. Matter 7 goes to the same clinical-governance professional already required for matters 1–2 and the same insurer or broker already required for matter 6 — an extra item on an existing agenda, not a separate workstream.

Why it's here: the baseline form's honesty depends on it not feeling watched; a 2-3 person room breaks the anonymity feel a large room provides by default.

BLOCK 2 — Understand the gap, learn the tool — 0:13–0:53

Mental fitness, defined — 0:13–0:22K-04 locked wording

What's taught: the exact locked definition of mental fitness, stated in full. How: delivered as a direct SAY line, word-for-word, not paraphrased. Why it's here: P18's coherence rule — if participants are meant to carry a definition out of the room, it has to be given to them exactly, not approximated.

Methods vs. the loop, by name — 0:13–0:22

What's taught: mindfulness, journalling, gratitude and similar named explicitly — real practices, with real research behind them, that build genuine capacity. Then the distinction, stated as a difference in conditions rather than a difference in worth: practising something when you have set the time aside is one set of conditions; reaching for it mid-conversation on a Tuesday is another. Same skill, different conditions. How: an explicit "keep doing it" framing, never a dismissal. Why it's here: without this, the room hears "another mindfulness session" and tunes out before the actual tool is taught — and the substantial share of any room who already practise something get spoken to directly rather than talked past. Softened 2026-08-27: this beat previously said the loop "decides whether you do anything at all in the moment that matters." That overstated the case in the same direction Foundations was corrected away from — mindfulness-based training does explicitly train informal, in-daily-life noticing, so implying other methods leave you with nothing in the moment is both inaccurate and needlessly adversarial. The two products now make the same argument at the same strength. If a participant says mindfulness does train that: they are right, and the facilitator agrees rather than defending — "Yeah, it does. So which moment this week do you want it in?"

The access gap — 0:13–0:22

What's taught: the problem isn't not knowing what to do, it's not being able to retrieve it under pressure, in the moment. How: a 60-second private prompt asking participants to recognise their own recent moment where this happened. Why it's here: reframes the whole session's purpose — this isn't new information, it's a new retrieval skill, which is why drilling (not just explaining) comes next.

The foundation — 0:13–0:22thoughts and feelings aren't instructions

What's taught: not every thought is a fact; feelings carry information but aren't orders. How: stated briefly, once, not dwelt on. Why it's here: underpins the whole loop — without this, "Shift" can be misread as suppressing a feeling rather than creating space around it.

Stop–Shift–Do, exact wording — 0:24–0:32locked

What's taught: the three-step tool in its exact locked wording, taught as the actual intervention the whole session exists to deliver. How: stated in full, then immediately scaffolded with colour and a worked example (below) rather than left as an abstract instruction. Why it's here: this is the deliverable — everything before it is groundwork, everything after it is retrieval-strengthening and rehearsal of this exact tool.

The colour scaffold, and its misreadings — 0:24–0:32

What's taught: red/amber/green mapped to the three steps, with each common misreading corrected in the same breath it's introduced. How: named once, cleanly, corrections folded directly into the initial teaching rather than left to be caught later. Why it's here: colour is a fast memory hook, but only if the wrong version doesn't take hold first.

Priya's Tuesday — desk version — 0:24–0:32added 2026-08-19, replaces disconnected examples

What's taught: one running character followed through a real desk-based moment where Stop-Shift-Do goes well. How: told as a small story, not a checklist of steps. Why it's here: narrative transportation carries content further than isolated case snippets.

Priya's Tuesday — non-desk version — 0:24–0:32added 2026-08-20, full parallel worked example

What's taught: the same character, a second moment that doesn't go well but still counts as a complete rep — and a full parallel non-desk-based scenario, so the tool doesn't read as office-only. How: told immediately alongside the desk version, explicit that a rep with an imperfect outcome still counts. Why it's here: Taylor et al. 2005's finding that mixed positive/negative modelling produces the greatest transfer; the non-desk parallel closes a real coverage gap for participants whose work or day doesn't look like a desk.

The retrieval drill — 0:32–0:39NEVER CUT, six-item call-and-response

What's taught: the tool drilled via six-item call-and-response, both directions, three corrections built in, ending on the point that outcome doesn't determine whether a rep counts as complete. How: low-risk, all response modes offered, nobody singled out. Why it's here: retrieval practice, not re-exposure, is what actually strengthens what's retained — this is the mechanism, not the explanation, that makes the tool retrievable under real pressure later.

Three misreads, performed as comedy — 0:39–0:43directed 2026-08-19, the session's licensed comic beat

What's taught: the facilitator hams up all three common misreadings of the tool, played for a laugh, with Priya brought back for a made-up "wrong version." How: genuine levity, the facilitator cast as the one who's wrong, never a participant. Why it's here: a facilitator willing to look foolish on purpose lowers the room's guard for the private, higher-stakes rehearsal still to come.

The forced recap — 0:39–0:43added 2026-08-20 after specialist review

What's taught: a quick, shouted-out group recap of all three corrections, run immediately after the comedy beat. How: fast, low-stakes group check, not individually verified. Why it's here: closes a real gap specialist review found — vivid, performed-wrong content resists correction without a follow-up retrieval cue right after it; without this beat the comedy risks teaching the wrong version more memorably than the right one.

The State Check — 0:43–0:46exact locked line

What's taught: "too much, too little, or workable" — run privately, once, in the participant's own exact locked wording. How: ten seconds, private, no sharing required. Why it's here: this is the trigger that decides whether the tool gets reached for in a real moment — without it, Stop-Shift-Do has no entry point.

The four shift routes — 0:46–0:51body, attention, perspective, connection

What's taught: the four places a Shift can work through, presented as a reference menu, explicitly not something to memorise — "in the moment it's three steps, always." How: given as a sheet to keep, not content drilled for retention under pressure. Why it's here: gives Shift somewhere concrete to go without overloading the core three-step tool with a full menu.

"Not staring at the feeling and waiting for it to change" — 0:46–0:51

What's taught: the specific correction on the attention route — turning toward what's happening, not fixating on the feeling itself. How: stated as a required line, not optional, every time the attention route is taught. Why it's here: the single most likely route to be misapplied as rumination if left uncorrected.

What's taught: a transition line moving participants from the easier rehearsed moment toward considering a genuinely harder one for their plan. How: a spoken bridge, not a written prompt. Why it's here: without an explicit push, participants default to planning around the easy moment they just rehearsed, not the harder one where the tool is actually needed.

How: written properly, on the card the participant keeps. Why it's here: implementation-intention evidence (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, d=0.65) is specific to this exact if-then structure, not a general intention.

What's taught: nothing verbal — fold the card once, hold it closed, real silence held in the room. How: private, physical, shared without anyone watching anyone else. Why it's here: the session's deliberate high point, placed after the cognitive work is done so it doesn't compete with writing the sentence. Status: real risk is delivery variance (a fumbled silence reads as dead air), flagged for room rehearsal with more than one facilitator; in a small room (2-5 people) a visible reaction during the hold is more exposed — genuinely open safety-adjacent question.

Why it's here: a participant who reads a lapse as failure stops trying; this is what keeps the door open to trying again after the first miss.

Why it's here: peak-end research says the ending carries disproportionate memory weight alongside the peak — engineering only one of the two would leave the other to chance.

The manager session — one hour, shared across both products, framed as the manager's own job to get good at.

Hunter's instruction, direct: "it needs its own product… we need them to fully be across it, to fully understand it… we can't just go tick in the box half an hour." A 45-minute session reads as a formality regardless of how well the content inside it is written. This is built as a proper meeting and a proper conversation — dialogue, not a briefing delivered at someone — with three jobs: what to actually watch for in the people they manage, how this gets built into team rhythms that already exist, and the honest limits of what a manager can and can't carry.

Structure follows HeadCoach (Gayed et al. 2024) — the manager-training design in this space with the strongest available RCT evidence on manager behaviour change (no exhaustive competitive audit has been run; MFC should not claim it is the only one) — which splits into two distinct tracks, notice/respond and prevent/design, taught in short scenario-based units rather than as one bundled block. The full Human Operating System is taught here, per Hunter's direction, because managers are the people who need to understand why the environment shapes what's available to someone — the individual sessions deliberately don't carry it.

Orientation, and the honest ceiling — 0:00–0:05

What's taught: what this hour is for, and what it can't do. Stated plainly, not softened: this is one hour against RESPECT's evidenced 4-hour dose, and Gayed et al.'s own meta-analysis found no significant pooled effect on employee symptoms even at full dose. The specific thing never to say, here or in any material: that training a manager protects their team's mental health — HeadCoach's RCT measured supervisor behaviour and confidence only, never outcomes for the people supervised. How: opens as a conversation, not a presentation — the register for the whole hour is set in the first two minutes. Why it's here: same claims discipline as every other format's opening, and it buys credibility with the sceptical manager in the room, who is usually the one worth convincing.

The framing the hour is built on, stated here and returned to at the close (D-0111, Hunter's own direction — this is not only "here's how this helps you personally"): "Some of what your people are about to learn is a responsibility you already carry, whether or not anyone named it. This hour is about what to be mindful of, and how to actually build that as a skill — the same way they're building theirs." Managers are not positioned as beneficiaries of the team's session, nor as its enforcers. They are positioned as people with their own thing to get good at.

What your team will actually experience — 0:05–0:12

What's taught: a factual run-through of the session their people will attend — its shape, not its content. Managers get enough to talk about it credibly without pre-empting anything the room needs to discover for itself. Includes the honest framing their team will hear: that this is a skill nobody was taught, not a deficiency to fix. Why it's here: a manager who mis-describes the session to their team can undo it before it starts — the most common version being "we're getting some mental health training," which is the exact misread the team session spends its first ten minutes correcting.

The Human Operating System, in full — 0:12–0:25Manager-only, per Hunter's direction

What's taught: the complete model — Driver, Passenger, Engine, plus Navigation, Pathways and Environment. This is the one place in MFC's whole delivery where the full six-part version is taught. Why here and nowhere else: Hunter's own reasoning — "we can actually bring in the whole entire operating system, which explains why the environment is so important… or what's actually happening within their employees, and this is why this is beneficial. But the individuals don't need to see and hear this in this session." A participant needs a tool they can reach for; a manager needs to understand why the same person has different capacity on different days, and what part of that sits inside their own control. The point that has to land: what's around someone shapes what's actually available to them. The manager is not being asked to fix that — they're being asked not to be the reason a pause costs something. Boundary: this is an explanatory model, not a diagnostic tool. Managers are never taught to assess or categorise a team member's state.

The six parts, as taught — the first three are what the team also learns, the last three are manager-only:

  • Driver — what a person can actually reach right now: noticing, pausing, choosing, making the next move on purpose. This is the part that varies most, day to day, in the same person.
  • Passenger — thoughts, feelings, urges, what someone wants and needs, how they're reading the situation. The Passenger is not bad, and this line is locked. A manager who treats a team member's frustration as the problem to remove has misread the model.
  • Engine — the body. Energy, tiredness, tension, sleep, food, recovery. The most under-rated of the six in a work setting: half of what gets read as attitude is an Engine state.
  • Navigation — what someone is actually steering by: what matters to them, what they're trying to protect or move toward. Two people in the same role can be navigating by completely different things, and it shows up as "unpredictable" behaviour until you know it.
  • Pathways — the routes that are well-worn for that person. What they default to under pressure because they've done it a thousand times. Not character; practice history.
  • Environment — everything around the person that makes a response cheap or expensive: workload, noise, psychological safety, whether pausing is visibly tolerated, whether the last person who raised something got heard. This is the only one of the six a manager has direct influence over, which is why the full model is taught here and not to individuals.

How it's taught, not lectured: the facilitator walks the six once, plainly, then asks the room a single question — "think of someone whose capacity has visibly changed over a few months; which of these six actually moved?" Managers answer out loud, in pairs or to the room. The point lands through their own example, not the model. Never ask anyone to name the person.

The manager-facing conclusion that this beat exists to reach: five of these six sit inside the person. One sits in the room you run. That is where a manager's actual leverage is — and it is also the honest limit of it.

Track one — notice and respond — 0:25–0:37HeadCoach, full text read

What's taught: what to actually watch for — signals, not categories. Changes from someone's own normal: withdrawal from things they usually join, a drop in follow-through that isn't like them, irritability that's new, someone going quiet in a way they weren't before. Explicitly not a symptom checklist and explicitly not diagnosis. Then the response: notice, ask directly rather than guess or hint, and point toward real support if it's more than a normal work conversation can hold. How: taught in short scenario-based units, mirroring HeadCoach's actual structure — a real situation, what you'd do, what you'd say. Managers work through two or three, out loud, in pairs or as a room. Not lectured. Why it's here: this is half of the design in this space with the strongest available behaviour-change RCT evidence, and it's the half managers most often have no language for.

The three scenarios, written out so the beat is runnable rather than described:

  • One. Someone who always contributes in your team meeting has said nothing for three weeks. Nothing else has obviously changed. What do you do — and when? The useful answer is usually earlier and smaller than managers expect.
  • Two. Someone tells you they’re “fine, just tired” for the third time, and you don’t think they are. What do you actually say next? Practise the sentence out loud — “you’ve said tired a few times now, how are you really going?” is a different act from “let me know if you need anything.”
  • Three. Someone tells you something clearly beyond a work conversation. What do you do in the next sixty seconds, and what do you not do? Do not counsel. Do not promise confidentiality you cannot keep. Do point to real support, and stay with them while they take the first step.

If the room is silent, or one senior voice dominates — the two most likely failure modes — the facilitator takes scenario one themselves, out loud, deliberately badly, then asks what they would have done differently. Modelling a wrong answer costs the room nothing to correct.

Track two — prevent, and design the rhythm — 0:37–0:50HeadCoach + implementation

What's taught: the preventive half — job demands, control, support, role clarity, communication — and then, concretely, where this actually lives in the week. Not a new meeting. Real options, chosen by the room: two minutes at the end of an existing team meeting; a standing slot that already exists (Hunter's example: a Friday); a physical or environmental reminder somewhere people already look. Managers pick which one fits their team, out loud, and say why. On role clarity, stated at its real strength: in an Australian construction-workforce profile analysis, the group with the lowest role clarity and highest demands also reported the highest burnout. That is a correlation in one sample, and role clarity there sits bundled with other factors — it is worth a manager checking whether their people actually know what's expected of them, and it is not a protective mechanism MFC can claim. Why it's here: the workplace-learning evidence points at ongoing, embedded conditions rather than one-off inputs — but that literature is about informal on-the-job learning, not transfer from a discrete training event, so it motivates the design without validating it.

The room rule, and what you never get to see — 0:50–0:53PROTECTED · restored as a taught beat 2026-08-27

What's taught: the privacy line, stated plainly to the person in the room with authority over the others. A manager never reads a team member's card, their written moment, or their cue sentence — not offered, not asked for, not "just curious." If a direct report is attending the team session, the facilitator is told in advance so the room can be run accordingly. How: said directly, in about two minutes, not buried in a handout. Why it's here: the team session runs on a privacy promise, and the fastest way to break it is a well-meaning manager asking to see what someone wrote. This was demoted to a document footnote in an earlier draft of this rebuild and restored on review — a rule nobody is told in the room is not a rule.

The one thing to actually run — 0:53–0:57PROTECTED, NEVER CUT · merged Crane + Kyndt

What's taught: one short, scripted, recurring prompt — not two separate mechanisms. Deliberately merged: asking a manager to run both a post-stressor reflection and a separate week-1/week-3 check-in is exactly the stacking that turns a simple idea into a programme nobody sustains. The prompt, folded into a check-in that already exists: "Something came up this week — how did you handle it, and what would you try next time?" Thirty seconds, whenever it's relevant, plus once deliberately in the first fortnight after the team session. How: written on the leave-behind card, rehearsed once in the room so the manager has said the words out loud before they need them. Why it's here: the supervisor-led after-action-review trial (Kho, Crane et al., randomised at the Royal Military College) found the supervisor-led group showed better outcomes earlier than training alone, with the effect mediated by perceived supervisor support. Two boundaries, both real: mental-health outcomes converged between groups by the final measurement point, so this is not a claim of durable effect; and a mediation finding does not establish that the content of the reflection is irrelevant — an earlier draft of this beat said "what matters is that the manager asked, not that they asked expertly," which the evidence does not license. Removed. Managers should run the prompt as written and take it seriously, not treat it as a box to tick.

Their own commitment, said out loud — 0:57–1:00PROTECTED, NEVER CUT

What's taught: one if-then sentence, modelled with an example, written privately, then read aloud once. Standard procedure, not conditional on anyone's preference. Plus the close: tell your team what you committed to — not merely that you attended — and the duty-discharge line restated plainly: this hour does not complete anyone's responsibility to their people. Why it's here: the same implementation-intention mechanism every other format uses, applied to the person with the most leverage in the room. Reading it aloud once is what makes it a commitment rather than a private intention, which is why the beat is protected. The known cost, disclosed: a manager may quietly soften what they wrote before it's heard, and nothing here checks for that.
