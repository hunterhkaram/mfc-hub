# Half-Day Foundations — what we know, what we do not, and how we will optimise it

## The honest status

**Hunter has not approved a final Half-Day workshop.** The 80-screen deck is the current internal
candidate. It is assembled, internally aligned and locally rendered, but Hunter has not been shown
and approved it as the strongest design. It has not completed the new design-intelligence stage,
timed room rehearsal, participant testing or qualified-human gates.

The previous review answered: **“Do the deck, guide and materials agree with the current recorded
decisions?”** It did not adequately answer: **“Is this the best available way to help these people
learn, remember and use mental fitness, and why?”** That distinction is now explicit and binding.

That is a strong input corpus. **It is not direct validation of this exact workshop.** The research

Most learning evidence supports principles, not a scientifically optimal slide count or precise
113:67 teaching-to-experience ratio.

## Three credible architectures now requiring comparison

| Approach | What it would feel like | Why it may be strong | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Current education-led sequence** | Understand the premise and model, retrieve it, then apply it | Reduces the risk that activity is performed without meaning; matches Hunter's rejection of the earlier practice-heavy cut | Too much explanation may consume attention and make 80 screens feel like a lecture |
| **B. Experience first** | Begin with a safe lived contrast, then explain what happened | Experience can create relevance and curiosity before terminology | The vault does not support assuming broad “productive failure”; an unguided opening may confuse or expose participants |
| **C. Recognition–experience–explanation spiral** | Repeated short cycles: familiar moment → micro-experience → explanation → retrieval → application | May preserve meaning while reducing long blocks and creating repeated encoding/retrieval opportunities | Could fragment the Framework or overload transitions if poorly facilitated |

The current package is **Option A, not the default winner**. The staged professional review must
compare these approaches and may recommend a hybrid. Slide count follows the chosen learning and
room architecture; it is never the design target.

## Load-bearing choices: current rationale and open challenge

| Choice | Current reason | Evidence status | What still has to be challenged or tested |
|---|---|---|---|
| Teach before extended practice | Hunter found the 44:56 cut removed the meaning while retaining activities | Founder judgement plus learning-design rationale | Compare against a spiral, not only the rejected old cut |
| Segment and signal content | Learning evidence supports segmenting, signalling, coherence and low-to-high fidelity | Principle-level evidence | Test the actual cognitive experience in the room; no evidence supplies a maximum slide count |
| Retrieval drill | Retrieval practice supports memory better than passive rehearing | Strong general learning principle; exact choral form not validated for MFC | Test dignity, energy, recall and inclusion with intended adults |
| Immediate applied rep | Mastery experience, feedback and contextualised practice plausibly support learning and self-efficacy | Adjacent/general evidence; not proof of real-world transfer | Test whether participants can perform the rep and later recognise a real opportunity |
| Radar cue and when–then plan | Implementation intentions and cueing are a plausible bridge from intention to action | Relevant mechanism evidence; MFC use untested | Check burden, comprehension and whether a cue survives beyond the room |
| Human Operating System teaching model | Creates a simple shared explanation of what can influence a moment | MFC framework/teaching judgement, not a brain map or validated model | Test whether it clarifies or adds avoidable conceptual load; justify its position and depth |
| Feelings as their own block | The response—not the existence of a feeling—is the trainable boundary underpinning later teaching | Founder/framework logic and safety relevance | Review wording clinically and test whether it lands without invalidation or therapeutic implication |
| Shared/team Radar | Attempts to connect individual practice to a group condition | Design inference from collective implementation ideas; routine measurement module is not approved | Test psychological safety, consent, facilitator capability, ownership and whether the host conditions exist |

## Important intelligence that had not reached the build clearly enough

1. **RULER's strongest lesson is not its branded tools; it is implementation architecture.** Adults
   and leaders learn and model the practices, tools remain visible in the environment, and repeated
   use is supported. Its evidence is largely developer-led, school-based and not proof for an
   Australian adult workplace workshop, so it is a comparator—not an answer.
2. **The market work recommends leaving reusable objects, not remembered ideas.** The current cue
   card and materials partly do this, but the design has not yet been challenged against a clear
   “what remains visible and usable on Monday?” standard.
3. **Stop–Shift–Do is not novel as a loop.** It sits beside DBT/MBSR STOP, RULER's Meta-Moment,
   ACT's Choice Point and similar interrupt–shift–act structures. MFC's defensible contribution is
   the integration, accessible language and moment-based practice architecture—not claiming to
   have invented the underlying loop.
4. **A single individual workshop cannot by itself establish organisational or population change.**
   Manager/leader support, shared practice conditions and host behaviour may be necessary. The
   Half-Day must be clear about the boundary between the participant product and a later
   implementation or organisational layer.
5. **Measurement cannot repair an unclear theory of change.** Each major beat should trace to a
   learning or behaviour mechanism and an intended observable signal. Attendance, satisfaction or
   one self-reported attempt cannot be called durable capability or social change.
