# Contrast activity asset specification, and plain-language readability pass

**Two deliverables in one file.**

**Deliverable 1** turns Beat 13's contrast activity (1:51–2:15) from a description into something a
producer or a facilitator can actually execute. Until now it existed as one sentence — *"a scenario
run without the skill, then the same scenario with it"* — in the design of record
(`01-three-hour-design.md:93`), the facilitator guide (`10-facilitator-guide-3hour.md:833`) and the
storyboard (`11-participant-materials-and-storyboard.md`, Beat 13). It is named in all three as the
highest-leverage stretch in the session and it is the only one of the eighteen beats that cannot be
delivered from the documents that exist. This closes that.

**Deliverable 2** is the plain-language readability pass on all ten participant materials in
`11-participant-materials-and-storyboard.md`. That document's own accessibility specification lists
*"No plain-language readability check has been run on the copy above. Required before print"* as the
first item in **§10 What this specification does not deliver**, and again in its open-gates table.
This performs that pass, with measured figures rather than asserted ones — and then states honestly
what a pass of this kind still cannot close.

**What this is not.** Approved. Pilot-ready. Safe to deliver. Nothing here touches `D-0016`, which
stands at **0 of 7**. Nothing here authorises participant-facing delivery, and Deliverable 2
explicitly **cannot** close the readability gate on Material 10 — see §2.14.

# DELIVERABLE 1 — The contrast activity, production-ready

## 1.0 What this asset has to do, and the constraints it is built inside

The beat's job is stated in the design and is narrow: **the gap is felt rather than described.**
Everything else in the session describes Stop–Shift–Do. This is the only place a participant
watches it happen at real speed, in a moment they recognise, and forms their own read of the
difference before anyone tells them what the difference was.

Binding constraints, carried from the governing records and not negotiable here:

| Constraint | Source |
|---|---|
| Same situation shown twice; the debrief question is *what did you notice*, not *what was right* | `11`, Beat 13 (**E**) |
| Context-neutral; no clinical content, no distress, no crisis | `11`, Beat 13 (**E**); `D-0005`; `K-08` rules 1–3 |
| Not distressing, not bereavement, not illness, not conflict escalation, nothing plausibly someone's live situation | `10`, 1:53–2:07 |
| The Do is **not** the perfect action; the outcome does not decide whether it counted | `K-07`; Mental Rep locked outcome-independence |
| Shift is **not** always calming down, and may be skipped | `K-07`; `K-08` rule 9; `D-0054` |
| Stop is **not** freezing | `K-07`; `D-0054`; Hunter's own instruction |
| No implication that struggle or difficulty accessing skills is a personal failure | `K-08` rule 3 |
| Captions and a transcript mandatory on any film or audio; no uncaptioned media | `11`, Beat 13 and Accessibility §5 |
| No causal, guarantee, treatment or outcome language | `K-08`; `11` claims sweep |
| No stock photography of distressed people, anywhere, ever | `11`, standing slide rules |
| Scenario content and medium are **flexible** (`D-0014`); the function is essential | `10` §1.3 |

Because scenario content is flexible, **this specification is a strong default, not a new essential
element.** A facilitator may substitute — within the constraints above and §1.5's prohibitions.
Nothing in this file creates a new `D-0014` essential element, and no decision record is required to
use a different scenario.

## 1.1 Timing — the fourteen minutes, allocated

`01` and `10` both allocate 1:53–2:07 to the contrast and 2:07–2:15 to the pair debrief. Within the
fourteen minutes:

| Clock | Duration | What happens |
|---|---:|---|
| 1:53:00 | 0:30 | Facilitator framing. *"Watch this. You're not judging it and there's no right answer to spot. Just watch."* |
| 1:53:30 | **2:40** | **Version 1 — without** |
| 1:56:10 | 0:30 | Reset. *"Same moment. Again."* Nothing else said. |
| 1:56:40 | **3:10** | **Version 2 — with** |
| 1:59:50 | 5:40 | Whole-room: *"Don't tell me what they did right. Tell me what you noticed was different."* Three or four contributions from volunteers. |
| 2:05:30 | 1:30 | Transition to pairs; buffer |
| **2:07:00** | | Pair debrief begins (§1.6) |

**Total film runtime, both versions: 5:50.** The two versions must sit **within 30 seconds of each
other**, and the difference must come from the pause itself, not from added dialogue. A "with"
version that is a minute longer teaches that using the skill takes a minute. It does not.

## 1.2 Scenario A — "The kettle" (primary)

### 1.2.1 Why this situation

It is the end of an ordinary day, in an ordinary kitchen, over an ordinary undone task. There is no
crisis, no conflict, no workplace, no illness, no third party at risk. The pressure is real but
small: accumulated tiredness meeting a reasonable question at the wrong second. Most adults have
been both people in it.

Critically, **the person who is asked the question has done nothing wrong** and is played with
complete neutrality. There is no antagonist. If the room can locate a villain, the scenario has
failed (§1.7).

### 1.2.2 Characters

| | |
|---|---|
| **Sam** | The person whose moment this is. Gender not specified. Age not specified. |
| **Jules** | The other person in the kitchen. Two lines. Gender not specified. |

**Casting and framing notes, binding:**

- **The relationship between Sam and Jules is never named and must not be inferable.** No wedding
  rings, no shared-bedroom cues, no photographs on the fridge, no pet names, no "love"/"mate"/"hon".
  They live in the same place. That is the entire specification. A viewer will supply partner,
  sibling, housemate, adult child, parent, friend — and every one of those readings works.
- **No children present, referenced, or audible.** Not because children are unsuitable, but because
  a child in frame instantly narrows the scenario to parents and adds a caretaking obligation that
  changes what the "without" version means.
- Cast Sam and Jules to reflect the room MFC is actually delivering to. Neither is written with any
  ethnicity, accent, class or body implied. Two performers of the same or different apparent age
  both work.
- **Sam is not a caricature of a stressed person.** No dishevelment, no sighing, no hand through
  hair, no bag dropped heavily. Sam is fine. That is the point. The tiredness is legible in tempo,
  not in performance.

### 1.2.3 VERSION 1 — WITHOUT

> **INT. KITCHEN — EARLY EVENING**
>
> *Sam comes in. Bag down on a chair. Coat still on. Fills the kettle at the tap, sets it on its
> base. Not hurried. Not slumped. The tempo of someone at the end of a long, unremarkable day.*
>
> *Jules is already in the kitchen, rinsing a mug. Not waiting for Sam. Barely looks up.*
>
> **JULES**
> Oh — did you send that form back?
>
> *Sam's hand goes to the kettle switch. Presses it. Does not turn round.*
>
> **SAM**
> No.
>
> *A beat. Still not turning round.*
>
> **SAM (CONT'D)**
> I've had a day.
>
> *Flat. Not shouted. Not cold enough to be a row. Just closed.*
>
> **JULES**
> Okay.
>
> *Jules takes the mug and goes. Not offended, not slamming anything — just gone.*
>
> *Sam stands at the counter. The kettle starts to build. Sam takes out their phone and looks at
> it. Knows exactly what just happened. Does nothing about it.*
>
> *Hold. Cut.*

**Running time 2:40.** Approximately half of that is Sam arriving and filling the kettle before
Jules speaks — this is deliberate, it establishes the tempo, and it must be identical in Version 2.

**What makes this sympathetic rather than contemptible:** Sam does not snap, insult, or escalate.
Sam gives a short flat answer while facing away, which is the most common thing in the world. The
camera does not judge it, no music comments on it, and Sam's final look at the phone is recognition,
not stupidity. A viewer's honest response should be *yeah, I've done that*, not *what an idiot*.

### 1.2.4 VERSION 2 — WITH

> **INT. KITCHEN — EARLY EVENING**
>
> *Identical. Same coat, same bag, same chair, same tap, same tempo.*
>
> **JULES**
> Oh — did you send that form back?
>
> *Sam's hand goes to the kettle switch —*
>
> *— and stops. Does not press it.*
>
> *(THE STOP. See §1.4 for how this is shot. Roughly two and a half seconds. Sam does not close
> their eyes, does not breathe theatrically, does not look up and away. The hand simply does not
> complete the movement.)*
>
> *Sam takes their hand off the switch. One out-breath — ordinary, the kind you don't notice
> yourself doing. Turns round and faces Jules.*
>
> **SAM**
> No, I didn't. Sorry — I'm not annoyed at you, I'm just fried. Can I do it after dinner?
>
> **JULES**
> Sure.
>
> *A small pause. It is still slightly awkward.*
>
> **JULES (CONT'D)**
> There's pasta left.
>
> *Jules takes the mug and goes.*
>
> *Sam stands at the counter. Presses the kettle. Takes out their phone and looks at it.*
>
> *Hold. Cut.*

**Running time 3:10.**

**The last shot is identical to Version 1 — same framing, same duration, same phone.** Nothing has
been rescued. Sam is still tired, still on their phone, and the form still is not sent. That
identical closing shot is the single most important craft decision in the asset, because it is what
stops the piece from teaching *use the skill and the evening gets better.*

**Dialogue alternates,** if "fried" will not land in the delivery region or with an EAL-heavy room —
substitute one of: *"I'm just wiped"*, *"I've got nothing left today"*, *"I'm just done in."* Do not
substitute anything that names an emotion as a label ("I'm feeling overwhelmed") — that is emotional
labelling, which `K-08` rule 8 and `K-06` push against, and it makes Sam sound like they have been
on the course.

### 1.2.5 The difference audit — proof that only the skill changed

Everything in the left column is **identical, frame for frame where filmed**:

| Held constant | |
|---|---|
| Location, dressing, props, time of day, lighting, lens, camera position | Identical setups |
| Both performers, wardrobe, hair | Identical |
| Sam's arrival, bag, coat, filling the kettle | Identical, same duration |
| Jules's line, tone, and delivery | Identical take where possible |
| Jules's exit with the mug | Identical |
| Sam's final action and final shot | Identical, same duration |
| Music | **None, in either version** |
| Narration, voiceover, on-screen text | **None, in either version** |

**Permitted differences — exactly four, and each is the skill:**

| # | Difference | What it is |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Sam's hand stops on the switch; ~2.5s before answering | **Stop** — catching the moment |
| 2 | Hand off the switch, one out-breath, turns to face Jules (~3s) | **Shift** — creating enough space; body and orientation. Visibly *not* calming down, and it takes three seconds |
| 3 | Sam's reply: 16 words instead of 5, facing Jules, naming the state and making a request | **Do** — the next move with intention |
| 4 | Jules adds *"There's pasta left"* | A **consequence** of 1–3, not a change to the setup |

Plus **two craft asymmetries**, both of which exist solely to make difference 1 visible, and both of
which are declared in §1.4 rather than smuggled in: the ambient-sound drop, and the unbroken shot
across the pause.

**If anything else differs, the contrast proves nothing** and the debrief will produce noticings
about lighting, mood or acting rather than about the moment.

## 1.3 Scenario B — "The cancellation" (alternate)

Provided because Scenario A assumes Sam lives with someone. Single-person households are a large
minority in both the UK and Australia (no figure is asserted here — this is a design assumption, not
a cited statistic), and in some delivery contexts — older-adult groups, community groups,
mixed open-enrolment rooms — a kitchen shared with another adult is not a neutral image. Scenario B
requires **one performer** and one prop, which also makes it the easier live role-play.

> **EXT./INT. BUS — LATE AFTERNOON**
>
> *Sam is on a bus, coat on, bag on knees, halfway across town. Phone in hand. Looking out of the
> window.*
>
> *The phone buzzes. A message from a friend: "I'm so sorry — I'm going to have to bail on today.
> Rain check?" It is the second time.*

**WITHOUT (2:40).** Sam reads it. Jaw sets, barely. Types with one thumb: **"fine."** Sends it.
Turns the phone face down on their knee. Looks out of the window. The bus keeps going. Sam does not
get off. Hold. Cut.

**WITH (3:10).** Identical up to the message. Sam reads it. The thumb goes to the keyboard — **and
stops.** *(THE STOP: ~2.5s, thumb hovering, the typing indicator not appearing.)* Sam looks up and
out of the window for two seconds — not a technique, just off the screen. *(The Shift: attention.)*
Then deletes what was there and types: **"Ah, that's a shame. Next week?"** Sends it. Turns the
phone face down on their knee. Looks out of the window. The bus keeps going. Sam does not get off.
Hold. Cut.

**Identical closing shot again**, and this one is even blunter: Sam is still halfway across town for
nothing, still disappointed, still on a bus. The only thing that changed is what got sent.

**Known adjacency, disclosed:** Material 6's worked example is also a message-avoidance scenario. It
is issued at 3:03, an hour after this beat, and is structurally different (days of not replying
versus sixty seconds after a cancellation). Scenario A avoids the adjacency entirely and is the
default for that reason; use B where a shared household cannot be assumed.

## 1.4 Route 1 — FILM

### 1.4.1 The hard problem: how to film a pause

A Stop is two seconds of nothing happening. Filmed naïvely it reads as an actor forgetting a line.
Filmed conventionally — slow motion, a music sting, a rack focus, a heartbeat on the soundtrack — it
reads as melodrama, and worse, it depicts *time stopping*, which teaches the exact misreading
`D-0054` exists to prevent: **Stop is not freezing.**

**Five techniques, all cheap, all required:**

1. **Give the pause a physical action to live inside.** Sam's hand goes to the switch and does not
   press it. The pause becomes an *interrupted movement*, not an absence of movement. The viewer's
   eye has somewhere to be. This is the whole solve; everything below supports it.
2. **Hold one unbroken shot across the pause.** Version 1's reactive beat is cut at roughly two-
   second intervals — propulsive, no room in it. Version 2 holds a single shot from Jules's line
   through Sam's reply, ~9 seconds unbroken. **The edit rate is the teaching.** Nobody in the room
   will be able to articulate this, and every one of them will feel it.
3. **Drop the room tone under the pause.** For ~2 seconds the kettle hum, the tap drip and the
   street outside come down 8–10 dB and Sam's own out-breath comes up. Restore on the reply. This is
   the most effective available rendering of an interior pause and it costs nothing in post.
   **Accessibility condition, binding:** because this is an audio-only cue it must be duplicated in
   the captions (`[room noise fades]` / `[room noise returns]`) and in the transcript, and the shot
   must hold long enough that a deaf viewer reading the caption still sees the held hand.
4. **Shoot Sam's hand and Sam's face in the same frame.** A cutaway to the hand tells the viewer
   where to look, which is the job the debrief is supposed to do. A medium shot that contains both
   lets each viewer notice whichever one they notice — which is what the prompt then asks about.
5. **Let Version 2 be slightly awkward.** The pause should be a beat too long for comfort. Real ones
   are.

**Prohibited entirely:** slow motion; freeze frame; ripple, blur, or desaturation effects; heartbeat
or breath sound design beyond the natural out-breath; any music in either version; voiceover of
Sam's thoughts; on-screen text of any kind, and in particular any **STOP / SHIFT / DO** caption over
the action. The on-screen labels are the most tempting and the most damaging: they convert an
observation task into a comprehension check and pre-empt the entire debrief.

### 1.4.4 Accessibility requirements — film

Owner: **A-12 (Ash)**. All of the following are conditions of use, not enhancements.

| Requirement | Specification |
|---|---|
| **Open captions** | **Burned in, as the default projected master.** Venue playback configuration is the single most common cause of an uncaptioned screening; burning them in removes the failure mode. A separate clean master plus `.srt` is supplied for the digital pack. |
| Caption quality | Verbatim, not paraphrased. Speaker-identified. Non-speech information captioned — `[kettle boiling]`, `[room noise fades]`, `[room noise returns]`, `[Sam breathes out]`. 1–2 lines, ≥ 20 characters/second reading rate not exceeded. Positioned so they never cover Sam's hand. |
| **Transcript** | Full transcript of both versions, including **action lines**, in the digital and plain-text packs. A transcript containing only dialogue is useless here — the asset is 90% action. |
| **Audio description** | Supplied as a **separate described version**, not mixed into the master. The AD script describes **observable action only, never inner state.** *"Her hand goes to the kettle switch. It stops. She takes her hand away. She breathes out. She turns round."* It must never say *"she pauses to think"*, *"she considers"*, *"she calms herself"* — that hands a blind participant the debrief answer the sighted room has to work out, and it asserts an interior state the film deliberately does not assert. |
| Photosensitivity | No flashing, no strobing, nothing above 3 flashes/second. Not a risk in this content; stated because it is a standing rule. |
| Contrast and legibility | Caption text ≥ 4.5:1 against its own background plate, checked against the **projected** image in the room, not on a laptop. |
| Volume | The room-tone drop must not require the master volume to be raised. Mix so the dialogue is intelligible at a comfortable level with the drop applied. |
| Alternative | If for any reason the film cannot be played **with captions**, the facilitator runs the live route instead. **No uncaptioned media may be used** (`11`, Accessibility §5). |

### 1.4.5 What must NOT be depicted — film

- Crying, shaking, hyperventilating, a raised voice, a slammed door, a thrown object.
- Any escalation. Jules must not react. Jules's "Okay" is neutral, and if the performance carries
  even mild hurt, the room watches a relationship being damaged instead of a moment being missed.
- Alcohol, cigarettes, medication, or any substance in frame.
- Children, either present or referenced.
- Any workplace, uniform, lanyard, laptop, or work call.
- Anything that reads as a clinical setting, a therapy room, a waiting room, a helpline number, or a
  mental-health poster on a wall. Dress the kitchen and check the background.
- A phone screen showing anything legible other than the message in Scenario B. No news, no social
  feed, no health app.
- Sam appearing serene, wise, or improved in Version 2. Sam is exactly as tired in both.
- Any suggestion Jules was owed the answer, or that Sam was in the wrong. The scenario has no fault
  in it.
- Any depiction of the skill as effortful mastery — no visible technique, no counted breathing, no
  closed eyes. `K-08` rule 6 bans forced breathwork and eye closure in the room; depicting them as
  the model is the same instruction one step removed.

## 1.5 Route 2 — LIVE ROLE-PLAY

This route is the **default assumption for pilot**, because MFC has no confirmed film budget and
because a live run is materially more flexible. It is not a downgrade: in a room of twelve, a
well-run live version is at least as strong as film, and the pause is easier to land live than on
camera because the room's own silence does half the work.

### 1.5.1 Who plays whom — four stagings, in order of preference

| | Staging | When | Notes |
|---:|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Two facilitators.** One plays Sam, one plays Jules. | Any session with a co-facilitator. **Strong default.** | Both rehearse. Jules's job is to be boring — same line, same tone, both times. The commonest live failure is a co-facilitator "helping" by playing Jules warmer in the second run, which destroys the difference audit. |
| **2** | **One facilitator + one briefed non-participant.** A host contact, a trainee facilitator, an MFC volunteer, briefed in advance. | Single-facilitator sessions with a host relationship. | A briefed non-participant is categorically different from a volunteer from the floor: they have seen the script, they have consented in advance, and they are not part of the group whose safety the session is promising. |
| **3** | **Solo, "read-then-play".** The facilitator stands **outside** a marked spot on the floor and reads the scene and Jules's line flatly from a card — *"Jules is at the sink. Jules says: oh, did you send that form back?"* — then steps **into** the spot and plays only Sam. | Single facilitator, no second person, no volunteer. **This is the standing fallback and it always works.** | This is a rehearsal-room convention (a staged reading), and rooms accept it without embarrassment because it is visibly not an attempt at drama. It also isolates the pause as the only performed content — which is exactly the only thing that needs performing. **Do not attempt to play both characters in character.** A single performer switching voices is a sketch, and the room will laugh. |
| **4** | **Participant volunteer.** | **Discouraged. See below.** | |

### 1.5.2 On asking a participant to perform — the honest position

**Do not build the activity on a participant volunteer.** Four reasons, and the fourth is the
governing one:

1. They cannot be briefed, so they will improvise, and improvisation drifts toward drama.
2. Whatever they improvise may be their own material. The scenario is designed to be recognisable;
   that is precisely why a participant performing it may not be performing.
3. The room watches a peer be watched. Even a willing volunteer changes the room's sense of what
   might be asked of them next.
4. **It sits against `D-0015`.** The session has promised, in writing on Material 1 and Material 10
   and verbally at 0:00, that *"you are never required to say anything personal out loud. Not
   once."* A public request for a volunteer to act out a moment converts that promise into a
   promise with an implied exception. The cost of one good live moment is not worth the cost of the
   room re-reading the promise.

**If a volunteer is used at all** — and this should be rare — then all of the following, together:
they play **Jules only** (two scripted lines, on a card, read not improvised); they are asked
privately at the break, never publicly from the front; the ask includes an explicit, easy no
(*"say no and I'll do it differently, it makes no odds to me"*); they never play Sam, and nobody is
ever asked to play the person being answered flatly in Version 1.

### 1.5.3 If nobody volunteers

**The design never reaches this state**, because staging 3 requires nobody. That is the answer, and
it is a design answer rather than a recovery answer.

Operationally: **do not ask twice.** Do not scan the room. Do not say *"anyone? …no? …okay"*, do not
say *"come on"*, and do not fill the gap with a joke about the room being quiet. A second ask
converts an invitation into pressure, and every participant present registers it. If an ask has
been made and nothing comes back within about three seconds, the correct move is a flat, unbothered
*"Fine — I'll do it myself, it's easier anyway,"* and straight into staging 3.

### 1.5.4 Full live script

**Staging.** Mark one spot on the floor with tape or a chair before the session — the "kitchen".
One real prop only: a mug, or a kettle if the venue has one. No coat, no bag, no set. The prop
matters because it gives the hand something to stop on; without a prop, the pause has nowhere to
live and the whole thing collapses into a facilitator standing still.

**[SAY] — the framing, before Version 1**

> I'm going to show you something twice. Two and a half minutes each.

### 1.5.5 How to run it without it becoming embarrassing theatre

Nine rules. Every one of them exists because breaking it is what makes a live demonstration cringe.

1. **Name it as a demonstration, not a performance.** The framing line above does this. Rooms
   forgive a demonstration anything and forgive a performance nothing.
2. **Play it smaller than you think.** Everything goes *down*: quieter than your facilitation
   voice, less movement, flatter. The instinct under mild embarrassment is to go bigger, and bigger
   is exactly what turns it into a sketch.
3. **No accents, no comedy, no exaggeration, no mugging.** Jules is not a nag. Sam is not a
   grump.
4. **One real prop, no set, no costume.** A coat and a bag are already costume.
5. **Never break the fourth wall mid-run.** Do not look at the room, do not comment, do not react
   to a laugh. If the room laughs during Version 1, keep going — the laugh is recognition and it is
   fine.
6. **Physically de-role.** Step into the spot to start, step out to finish. That single visible step
   is what tells the room the flat answer was not the facilitator's personality. Facilitators who
   skip it get read as having been genuinely short-tempered.
7. **If it goes wrong, do not rescue it with a joke.** Say *"let me run that again"*, flatly, and
   run it again. A joke tells the room the content was embarrassing, and by extension that their own
   version of this moment is embarrassing. That is the opposite of the session's whole frame.
8. **Rehearse the pause with a timer**, out loud, three times, before the session. It is the only
   part of the entire facilitator guide that cannot be delivered cold.
9. **Do not narrate over either version.** Already required at `10`:1:53–2:07. Restated because the
   temptation is strongest in the live route, where the silence belongs to you.

**Add to the rehearsal checklist (§11 of `10-facilitator-guide-3hour.md`):** *"The two-second pause
in the contrast activity, timed, three times."*

## 1.6 The debrief — eight minutes, 2:07–2:15

### 1.6.1 The governing instruction

**Participants are asked what they NOTICED, not what was correct.** This is marked **E** in the
storyboard and it is not a stylistic preference — it is the difference between a felt-gap activity
and a comprehension check. A comprehension check at this point would teach the room that there is a
right way to have watched, which retroactively converts the two versions into a worked example and
destroys the only beat in the session where a participant forms their own read of the mechanism.

**Corollary, binding on the facilitator: no noticing is confirmed, graded, corrected, or ranked.**
Not with words, not with "yes, exactly", not with a nod that is bigger for one answer than another.

### 1.6.2 Allocation

| Clock | Duration | |
|---|---:|---|
| 2:07:00 | 0:30 | Instruction and the "about the scenario, not yourselves" guard |
| 2:07:30 | 6:00 | Pairs, three questions |
| 2:13:30 | 1:30 | Two answers to question 3 pulled into the room; one-sentence link back; close |

### 1.6.3 Exact prompts

**[SAY] — the instruction**

> Pairs. Eight minutes. Whoever's next to you — and if you'd rather not, that's completely fine,
> sit with it on your own.
>
> You're talking about **the scenario**, not about yourselves. Nothing personal is required here and
> nothing you say goes anywhere.

**[DO]** Put the three questions on screen, all three at once, and leave them up for the whole six
minutes. Participants who lose the thread should be able to find it without asking.

> **1. Where exactly was the gap?**
> Point at the second it happened, not the general feeling of it.
>
> **2. What did it cost them to take it?**
> Because it cost something.
>
> **3. What would make that gap harder to find in real life?**

**[DO]** Circulate. Do not sit down with a pair — a facilitator joining a pair makes that pair's
conversation the assessed one. Six minutes.

### 1.6.4 Note on question 1

Question 1 **presupposes there was a gap**, and that is a deliberate, disclosed choice rather than
an oversight. The open question has already been asked — the whole-room prompt at 1:59:50 is *"tell
me what you noticed was different"*, with no presupposition in it at all. Question 1 focuses the
pair conversation after the open phase has run.

**But the presupposition must not become a requirement.** If a participant says *"I didn't see one"*
or *"there wasn't a gap"*, that is a legitimate answer and is welcomed, not corrected:

> **[SAY]** Fair enough — where did you expect one to be? That's the more interesting question
> anyway.

### 1.6.5 Scripted responses to the four predictable answers

These are the four things rooms actually say. Each has one correct handling and at least one
tempting wrong one.

| The room says | **[SAY]** | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *"They were calmer."* | *"They were — although calmer isn't the target. What actually changed is that there was a gap. Calm is just what this particular gap happened to look like."* | `K-08` rule 9; `D-0054`'s amber correction. This is the highest-frequency answer and letting it stand teaches "Shift = calm down". Carried verbatim from `10`. |
| *"They did it right the second time."* / *"That's what you're supposed to do."* | *"There isn't a right version. The second one isn't better behaviour — it's the same person with about three seconds of room in it. What did you actually see change?"* | The comprehension-check failure mode. Do not agree, and do not praise the observation. |
| *"That's not realistic — nobody does that."* | *"Probably right. Most of the time this person does version one. So does everyone in here. What we're looking at is one moment going differently, not a person being different."* | **This is the single most valuable contribution the room can make and it must never be argued down.** It is also the exact honesty of Material 6's closing paragraph. A facilitator who defends the scenario here has started selling. |
| *"Nothing was different."* | *"Okay — genuinely useful. Point at the second where you expected something and didn't get it."* | Accept it. Then use it. |

**One further handling, for the answer that carries risk.** If anyone starts to describe their own
version of this moment in the whole-room phase, the facilitator redirects immediately and warmly to
the scenario — *"I'll stop you there only because we're keeping this about them, not us — what did
you notice in the second one?"* The debrief is a `D-0015` private-by-design beat (`11`,
Accessibility §7) and the drift into personal disclosure is the failure it is designed against.

**[DO]** Pull **two** answers to question 3 into the room. They will name tiredness, other people

**[SAY] — one sentence, then stop**

**[DO]** **Do not re-teach the Human Operating System.** The whole value of this moment is that the

## 1.7 What would make this asset fail

Named honestly, because each of these is a real and likely outcome of ordinary good intentions, not
an exotic risk.

**1. Melodrama.** The most likely failure by a distance. It arrives through craft: a music cue under
the pause, slow motion, a close-up on a trembling hand, a performer playing *tired* rather than
being tired. It also arrives live, through a facilitator going bigger when they feel exposed. The
symptom is that the room watches a *story* instead of a *moment*, and the noticings come back as
"it was sad" rather than "there was a second where nothing happened." **Countered by:** no music in
either version, identical treatment (§1.2.5), the prohibitions in §1.4.1, and live rule 2.

**2. An implausibly serene "with" version.** Sam becomes a person who has done the course. They
breathe deliberately, speak warmly, and the evening improves. This teaches that the skill produces
composure and good outcomes, which contradicts `K-07` on three separate locks — Shift is not always
calming down, Do is not the perfect action, and the mental rep's outcome-independence — and drifts
straight into `K-08`'s guaranteed-outcome territory. **Countered by:** the identical closing shot,
the deliberately awkward pause after "Sure", the three-second Shift, and the "that's not realistic"
handling in §1.6.5. **Named contingency:** if pilot feedback reads Version 2 as too resolved, the
permitted trim is Jules's *"There's pasta left"* line. Cut it and Jules simply leaves.

**3. A "without" version that invites laughing at the character.** If Sam is written or played as
oblivious, petulant, or stupid, the room gets to feel superior — and the session's entire
non-shaming frame, built at 0:38 with *"that's a gap in what we're taught, not a gap in you"*,
collapses in one beat. It is worse than useless: a participant who laughs at Sam has been taught
that the moment they recognise in themselves is contemptible. **Countered by:** Sam does not snap,
Jules does not react, the flat answer is the most common thing in the world, and the closing shot is
recognition rather than ignorance. **Test:** show Version 1 alone to someone outside the project. If
their first response is anything other than a version of *"yeah, I've done that"*, it fails.

**4. A scenario that only lands for one demographic.** Scenario A assumes a shared household;
Scenario B assumes a phone and a friend. Neither is universal. A kitchen scenario in a room of
people who live alone, or a bus scenario in a rural room where nobody uses public transport, produces
polite blankness — which is indistinguishable from the activity working, because the whole beat is
silent. **Countered by:** two scenarios with different assumptions; the deliberately unnamed
relationship; no children, no workplace, no car, no named place; and the facilitator's `D-0014`
freedom to substitute. **Not countered:** `F7` cross-cultural applicability remains open and
unresolved, and this asset does not close it. A scenario built and reviewed inside one cultural
frame is a mitigation, not a resolution.

**5. Anything implying the skill guarantees a good outcome.** The failure is rarely a sentence — it
is usually structural: the "with" version ends better than the "without" version, and the room draws
the obvious conclusion without anyone having claimed anything. This would be a `K-08` breach
delivered entirely through editing. **Countered by:** the identical final shot, identical final
duration, and the fact that in both versions the form is still not sent, Sam is still tired, and Sam
still ends up looking at their phone.

**6. On-screen labels.** Adding **STOP / SHIFT / DO** captions over the action, or a facilitator
narrating *"there's the Stop"*, is the single most tempting improvement anyone will propose. It
converts the only observation task in the session into a labelling task, guarantees the debrief
produces recall rather than noticing, and makes the eight-minute pair debrief redundant. **This is
prohibited in both routes.**

**7. Two versions that differ in more than the skill.** Different lighting because the second was
shot after lunch; a warmer Jules because the co-facilitator was being helpful; a longer "with"
version because the actor found more in it. Each is invisible individually and each confounds the
contrast. **Countered by:** the difference audit in §1.2.5, which should be checked against the
final cut, not just against the script.

# DELIVERABLE 2 — Plain-language readability pass, all ten participant materials

## 2.0 Method, and what was actually measured

**What was done.** The participant-facing copy of each of the ten materials — the blockquoted text
only, not the fidelity notes or design notes, which participants never see — was extracted
programmatically and scored for Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level, Flesch Reading Ease, Gunning Fog, average
sentence length, average syllables per word, and polysyllabic-word density. Every sentence over
20 words was listed and inspected individually. Em-dash and contraction density were counted
separately. The whole corpus was then read line by line by A-08 (Nova) and A-12 (Ash) for the things
formulae cannot see: idiom, ellipsis, register clash, jargon leakage, and constructions that are
short and simple and still opaque.

**Two figures are reported for each material, deliberately.** Headings, table cells, form labels and
write-in lines are not sentences, and including them inflates sentence length. Excluding them,
however, hides real reading burden — Material 5's six-component table is the densest text on any
participant material and a participant has to read it. Where the two figures differ materially, both
are given.

**Target.** `11`'s own accessibility specification §1: *"readable by an adult reading at roughly
UK/AU school Year 6–8 level"*, average sentence length ≤ 15 words on cards and ≤ 20 on sheets, hard
cap 25 words, zero clinical or clinical-adjacent vocabulary, minimal idiom, no abbreviations.
Flesch–Kincaid grade **6–8** is the corresponding band.

## 2.15 System-level findings across all ten materials

Five patterns that no single per-material edit addresses.

**S1 · The em-dash parenthetical is the corpus's dominant rhythm, and it is the biggest single
readability lever available.** Main clause — qualifier — resumption, across all ten materials, at up
to 3.4 per 100 words. It is excellent prose and it is a known barrier: low-literacy readers lose the
main clause across the interruption, and screen readers vary in whether they pause at an em-dash at
all (several read it as nothing, joining two clauses into one run-on). **Recommendation:** a single
global pass converting roughly one em-dash in three to a full stop, prioritising (a) any sentence
over 20 words, (b) every safeguarding line on Material 10, and (c) any sentence where the payload is
*behind* the dash. Most of the individual rewrites above are instances of this one move.

## 2.18 What a synthetic readability pass cannot establish

Stated plainly, because the gate this closes is narrower than its name suggests, and `11`'s own
accessibility spec §10 sets the standard for this kind of disclosure.

1. **This is not a user test.** No participant, of any literacy level, has read a single one of these
   materials. Everything above is a model of a reader, produced by a system whose own reading is
   nothing like the reading being modelled. A twenty-minute session with four adults at the target
   literacy level would find things this pass did not, and would contradict some of it.

2. **Readability formulae measure word and sentence length, and nothing else.** Flesch–Kincaid
   cannot see idiom, ambiguity, cultural assumption, register clash, missing referents, or the
   difference between a familiar long word and an unfamiliar short one. §2.6 is the proof: the
   highest-scoring material in the corpus needed four edits. **Do not report the grade levels in
   §2.1 as the result of this pass.** The findings are the result; the numbers are the least
   informative part of them.

3. **`11`'s own spec requires a pass "run by someone who did not write them."** A-08 and A-12 are
   credited co-authors of the materials being assessed here. This pass is therefore **less
   independent than the specification asks for**, and the spec's requirement is **not** discharged
   by it. It should be treated as a thorough author-side pass that surfaces most defects cheaply,
   with a genuinely independent pass still owed.

4. **Reading conditions are not modelled.** These materials are read standing up, in a room with
   strangers, at 2h53 into a three-hour session, possibly after a poor night's sleep, on paper whose
   final typography, contrast and stock are not yet chosen. Material 8's real burden is much higher
   than grade 4.9 for that reason. Effective readability is a product of copy **and** typography
   **and** timing, and this pass only touched the first.

5. **EAL flags are inferences, not observations.** Every "an EAL reader would stumble here" above is
   a judgement about a category of reader, not a report of anyone stumbling. EAL is also not one
   population: a Portuguese speaker, a Mandarin speaker and an Arabic speaker will find different
   things hard, and idioms that are transparent in one first language are opaque in another.

6. **Nothing here speaks to Easy Read.** `11` records the absence of an Easy Read version as an open
   gap, and this pass does not close, narrow, or substitute for it. Plain English at Year 6–8 and
   Easy Read are different products for different readers.

7. **Cross-cultural applicability (`F7`) remains open.** Several findings above are about UK/AU idiom
   specifically, which is a symptom of the same unresolved question and not a treatment for it.

8. **Material 10 cannot be signed off at all** — §2.14.

**Recommended real test, when `D-0016` allows one.** Six adults, recruited for a spread of literacy
and English-language background, none connected to MFC. Each is given the pack cold and asked to
read aloud and say what each material is asking them to do. Record where they stop, re-read, or
guess. That is thirty minutes per person and it will find more than everything above.
