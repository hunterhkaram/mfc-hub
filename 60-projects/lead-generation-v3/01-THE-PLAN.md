## 2.3 · E3 — what MFC leaves with

**The mechanism: the person messages MFC one word from their own phone. MFC captures nothing.**

**Why this shape and not a QR code to a form.** A QR code needs a page — and the live site has no
product page and `/radar` is a 404. A form needs a collection notice and a privacy position MFC does
not have. A paper sign-up sheet is the worst of the three: MFC transcribes personal information it
cannot yet justify holding (`AG-016`, Debra: *"you've got to have a justification for holding on to
someone's personal information"*). **An inbound message is the only contact that arrives with its own
consent already attached, needs no website, no wifi and no page, and works from a phone in a park.**

### ⚑ The keyword is per channel, not one word for everything

head-of-digital's finding: the plan invented a capture mechanism that is a *word*, then used the same
word everywhere — so a chamber breakfast and a Men's Shed were indistinguishable in the inbox, and
`how_found` (free text, answered by the person who remembers least) was the only attribution MFC had.
**Zero cost to fix; it is a decision, not a build.** head-of-product independently found the same word
was welded to the desk scenario, which only runs in professional rooms. Both are answered by the same
change:

| Channel | Word | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Professional rooms** | **`WEDNESDAY`** | The scenario the room has just spent three minutes inside. A memory of the content, not a code |
| **Community rooms** | **`ATTEMPT`** | ⚑ Those rooms run a different scenario, so `WEDNESDAY` would mean nothing. **`ATTEMPT` is room-independent and rehearses the one sentence that must survive to Thursday** |
| **Social and inbound** | **`NOTICE`** | Separates self-sourced inbound from room-sourced in the sheet |

**What he says, exactly.** ⚑ **The photo comes first.**

> *"Two things and then I'm out of your way. One — take a photo of this, now, while I'm standing
> here."* **(Holds up the printed A5 card. Waits. Actually waits.)** *"Photos are free and you can
> delete it in the car.*
>
> *Two — the one-pager with this rep on it is in the highlight on that account called **THE REP**. You
> don't need to message me at all. If you'd rather I sent it to you, or you look after a team,
> message me the word **[WEDNESDAY / ATTEMPT]**. That's the only thing I'll ever send you. If you want
> something after that you can ask, and if you don't, you'll never hear from me again."*

**Two changes from the first draft, on head-of-marketing's H2 finding (§7.1 M3):**

- **Photo first, message second.** Almost nobody sends a message to a stranger while standing in a
  room. Almost everybody will take a photo when told to, because it costs nothing and commits to
  nothing. **The photo is the act that survives the room; the message happens that night on the
  couch.** Asking for the message alone asks for the hardest act at the least likely moment.
- **The word is `WEDNESDAY`, not `REP`.** `REP` is MFC's internal vocabulary and, said aloud to a run
  club, is a gym word. `WEDNESDAY` is the scenario the room has just spent three minutes inside —
  *twenty to four on a Wednesday* — so the word is a memory of the content rather than a code, and it
  will still mean something to a person opening Instagram nine hours later. It is also unambiguous
  in a DM inbox, which `REP` is not.

**Said twice** — once here, once to anyone who comes up afterwards. **And the handle is said aloud
twice, slowly**, because the card is not legible past the front rows of a 200-person run club.

### ⚑ The `THE REP` highlight, and its honest tradeoff

head-of-digital's design change, adopted: **put the one-pager in a public Story Highlight so the
low-intent majority get the content with no DM at all.**

- **Gain:** the people who *do* message are self-selected higher intent — which is exactly where X2
  (can they open a door) is weakest. It also protects the 0.75-hour follow-up block at the volumes X1
  assumes, and it means a person who sends the word gets the thing in four seconds while the human
  reply becomes a conversation rather than a delivery.
- **Cost, stated:** **X1 falls and X2 should rise.** Some people who would have messaged for the
  one-pager now simply take it and are never contacts.
- **Whether that is a better product is an empirical question the first three rooms answer.** **Record
  both numbers separately from room 1** so the tradeoff is measured rather than argued.
- Cost to build: ~1 hour, once. No page, no privacy determination, works offline-later.

# 4 · SOCIAL (S)

## 4.1 · S1 — the evidence question, answered before the cadence is set

Hunter's own framing: *"don't want to be too active — although open to it if evidence supports this
move/approach."* **So the evidence decides it, and the honest answer is not the flattering one.**

> ### The answer: **No. The evidence does not support that posting more produces leads for an organisation in MFC's position.** What it does support is a *floor* — below roughly two to three posts a week, reach measurably degrades. And reach is not the bottleneck MFC has.

### What was actually found, for and against

| | Finding | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| **FOR** | Buffer's analysis of ~2 million Instagram posts: reach per post rises with frequency, with the biggest jump from 1–2 to 3–5 posts a week and **clearly diminishing returns beyond that** (~20–25% incremental). | **EVIDENCE by sample size; vendor-owned data by source.** The largest sample found. |
| **FOR** | Multiple independent vendor sources converge: an account that goes silent underperforms its own baseline. "Any posting beats none." | **VENDOR CLAIM**, directionally corroborated across sources, none independently verified |
| **FOR** | LinkedIn vendor consensus: 3–5 posts a week is optimal, with diminishing returns past that and a penalty for two same-day posts. | **VENDOR CLAIM** |
| **AGAINST** | **Every source that measures frequency measures it against reach or engagement. Not one measures it against enquiries, conversations or sales.** Every source that measures *conversion* (LinkedIn 2.74%, Instagram ~1%, paid social 0.9%) attributes it to channel and offer specificity, never to volume. | **The absence is the finding**, and it is where the question actually lives |
| **AGAINST** | Education-sector LinkedIn data: highest engagement at **2–3 posts a week**; posting more did not improve it. | VENDOR CLAIM, uncited primary — but it points the same way |
| **AGAINST** | The one operator-level data point found (a small B2C wellness account, ~2 years of its own Insights, N=1): **click-through rate, not follower count or frequency, tracked with enquiries.** Its author states directly that when results lack enquiries, *"the issue is rarely the posting frequency."* | **WEAK — N=1, self-reported, wrong vertical (B2C not B2B).** Reported because it is the most operationally relevant thing found, not because it is strong |
| **AGAINST — and this is the structural one** | Ehrenberg-Bass / LinkedIn B2B Institute: growth comes from **penetration** — reaching more actual category buyers — not from **intensity** of exposure to an existing follower base. | **Marketing-science tier.** The strongest reasoning available |
| **NOT FOUND** | A direct search for evidence that charity or nonprofit organic social converts followers into paid B2B service buyers returned **nothing**, vendor or academic. Search terms recorded. | **A real gap, stated rather than filled** |

### Why the structural argument decides it for MFC specifically

**MFC's 8,800 Instagram followers are very unlikely to be the people who buy a $4,850 workshop.** They
are an audience assembled by a charity's mission content, not by a procurement need.

⚠ **That sentence is the load-bearing claim of this whole section and it has never been checked** —
head-of-social's H2 finding (§7.1 S6). **It is an assertion, not a measurement, and it is checkable in
ten minutes inside the Instagram app**: follower geography, the share of followers on business
accounts, and the top-city split. **That check is a first-week task in §4.2 and is not optional.** If a
material share of the 8,800 turn out to be Australian employers, the conclusion of §4.1 changes and the
cadence recommendation should be re-taken. Posting to them
more often increases exposure to people who are structurally not on the buying committee. **More
frequency does not expand the addressable buyer set. A different room does.** That is exactly what §2
of this plan is, and it is why social gets half an hour and rooms get two and a half.

**Against MFC's own prior position** (`R1` Part 4; `STRATEGY-2026-08-24` §5), which said personal-profile

## 4.4 · S3 — comment-to-reply capture: ruled

> ### Ruling: **do not build it.** Not now, and not at this volume.

The mechanism was designed in `STRATEGY-2026-08-24` §5 and never built. **It should stay unbuilt, and
the reason is arithmetic, not caution:** this plan produces roughly **fifteen inbound contacts a
month.** An automation is engineering, a monthly subscription, a keyword taxonomy and a distress
blocklist, in exchange for saving a person perhaps forty minutes a month — at a volume where a human
reply is both feasible and materially better than a machine one.

**And the risk is asymmetric and specific.** The failure the original design named is real: someone
comments *"I really needed this — I've been struggling since my brother died,"* and a machine replies
with a link. **A mental-health charity answering grief with a marketing message, in public,
screenshottable.** There is a quieter version that is arguably worse — the automation works perfectly,
and the person believes MFC saw their distress and responded, when no human saw it at all.

**What replaces it: a human, within two business days** (§2.3). That is the same promise, delivered
better, at seventeen a month.

**The requirements, recorded so this is a decision and not an omission** — if inbound ever exceeds
roughly **eighty messages a month**, which is the point at which a human reply stops being feasible in
the follow-up hour, the mechanism gets built to this specification and no looser:

1. **Keyword-gated trigger only** — fires on an exact word the person was explicitly asked to comment. **Never on any comment. Never on sentiment.**
2. **A distress-language blocklist that suppresses the automation** and routes to a human queue instead.
3. **The message discloses that it is automated and that a person reads replies.**
4. **One message that delivers the thing, then stops.** No sequence.
5. **A named human checking comments daily.**
6. **The profile states that MFC does not monitor comments or DMs for distress and cannot respond to a crisis** — required whether or not the automation is ever built, and already required by §2.3.
7. **No automation on LinkedIn.** Their user agreement prohibits it and they enforce it against accounts.
8. **A privacy determination in place before any list is built from it.**
